## 1. What do sampling theory and the texture-filtering literature say about residual aliasing of trilinear/anisotropic filtering, and how does boosting high frequencies in level 0 change it?

### Takeaway
The normative model of GPU anisotropic filtering is an unweighted average of N bilinear/trilinear probes along the footprint's major axis at a mip level chosen from the *minor* axis; this is a box filter along one axis built on box-filtered mips, i.e. a weak low-pass with large side lobes, and the specs explicitly leave the real scheme implementation-dependent. Peer-reviewed work (Mavridis & Papaioannou 2011, abstract seen as snippet only) states that hardware filtering still shows spatial *and temporal* aliasing; sharpening level 0 raises exactly the frequency band that this weak filter must suppress, so any residual aliasing scales up with the sharpening gain. I could not open the classic papers to quote their own statements.

### Cited Findings
- **THEORY (normative spec).** Khronos, *EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic* (OpenGL extension spec, NVIDIA et al., originally 1999/2000; revision in the Khronos OpenGL-Registry). The example scheme: `Px = sqrt(dudx^2 + dvdx^2)`, `Py = sqrt(dudy^2 + dvdy^2)`, `Pmax = max(Px,Py)`, `Pmin = min(Px,Py)`, `N = min(ceil(Pmax/Pmin), maxAniso)`, `Lamda' = log2(Pmax/N)`. "Instead of a single sample, Tau, at (u,v,Lamda), 'N' locations in the mipmap at LOD Lamda, are sampled within the texture footprint of the pixel" and the result is `TauAniso = 1/N * sum(Tau(...))`, i.e. an **equal-weight (box) average** of N isotropic samples spaced along the major axis. — [EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/OpenGL-Registry/main/extensions/EXT/EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt)
- **THEORY (normative spec).** Same spec: "The particular scheme for anisotropic texture filtering is implementation dependent." It also permits rounding N up ("an implementation may only support power-of-two sampling rates") and approximating Px/Py by cheaper functions Fx/Fy bounded by `max(|du/dx|,|dv/dx|) <= Fx <= |du/dx| + |dv/dx|`. So the footprint estimate itself may be off by up to a factor sqrt(2)-ish and the spec gives no quality guarantee. — [EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/OpenGL-Registry/main/extensions/EXT/EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt)
- **THEORY (normative spec).** Khronos, *Vulkan Specification*, chapter "Image Operations" (`textures.adoc`, current main branch 2026): `eta = min(rho_max/rho_min, maxAniso)`, `N = ceil(eta)`, "An implementation may round N up to the nearest supported sampling rate. An implementation may use the value of N as an approximation of eta." `lambda_base = log2(rho_max/eta)`. Anisotropic filter: "Instead of a single isotropic sample, N isotropic samples are sampled within the image footprint of the image level d to approximate an anisotropic filter", again a `1/N` sum. "The particular scheme for anisotropic texture filtering is implementation-dependent." It adds: "However, anytime the footprint is small in texel space the implementation may use a smaller value of eta". — [Vulkan-Docs textures.adoc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/Vulkan-Docs/main/chapters/textures.adoc)
- **THEORY (normative spec).** Vulkan spec note: "For historical reasons, vendor implementations of anisotropic filtering interpret these sampler parameters in different ways ... Applications should not expect consistent behavior in such cases". — [Vulkan-Docs textures.adoc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/Vulkan-Docs/main/chapters/textures.adoc)
- **CODE.** Google SwiftShader (software Vulkan implementation), `SamplerCore::computeLod2D` and `sampleAniso`: `lod` starts as the squared major-axis length; `anisotropy = lod / det` (major^2 / |Jacobian determinant|, about major/minor), clamped to `maxAnisotropy`; then `lod *= 1/(anisotropy*anisotropy)`, i.e. LOD from major/N; `N = RoundInt(anisotropy)` equally weighted probes are accumulated along the major-axis delta. A real, shipping implementation that follows the spec's box-of-probes model literally. — [SwiftShader SamplerCore.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/swiftshader/master/src/Pipeline/SamplerCore.cpp)
- **CODE (reference offline renderer).** Pharr, Jakob, Humphreys, *pbrt-v4* source (companion to "Physically Based Rendering", 4th ed., 2023). `Image::GeneratePyramid` builds each level as the plain average of 4 source pixels (`(a+b+c+d)/4`, a 2x2 box). `MIPMap::Filter` for EWA: sorts the two footprint vectors, clamps the ratio ("Clamp ellipse vector ratio if too large": if `shorter * maxAnisotropy < longer` the *shorter* axis is lengthened), then picks `lod = max(0, Levels()-1 + Log2(shorterVecLength))` and lerps two EWA lookups. The trilinear path instead uses the *largest* derivative component (`width = 2*max(|dst0|,|dst1| components)`) to pick the level. So even the reference offline renderer uses box-filtered pyramids and selects the level from the minor axis for anisotropic lookups, compensating with a Gaussian-weighted (EWA) kernel rather than a box of probes. — [pbrt-v4 mipmap.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mmp/pbrt-v4/master/src/pbrt/util/mipmap.cpp), [pbrt-v4 image.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mmp/pbrt-v4/master/src/pbrt/util/image.cpp)
- **MEASURED/peer-reviewed claim, `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** Mavridis, P. & Papaioannou, G., "High Quality Elliptical Texture Filtering on GPU", ACM SIGGRAPH Symposium on Interactive 3D Graphics and Games (I3D) 2011, DOI 10.1145/1944745.1944749. The abstract as returned by search: "the quality of available hardware texture filtering, even on state-of-the-art graphics hardware, suffers from several aliasing artifacts, in both spatial and temporal domains"; the paper builds an EWA approximation from hardware anisotropic probes. I could not open the paper; the evidence in it (as far as the abstract indicates) is image comparison, not a psychophysical measurement. — [hgpu.org entry (not reachable from this session)](https://hgpu.org/?p=3192), DOI [10.1145/1944745.1944749](https://doi.org/10.1145/1944745.1944749)
- **Peer-reviewed claim, `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** Manson, J. & Schaefer, S., "Cardinality-Constrained Texture Filtering", ACM TOG (SIGGRAPH) 2013: search snippet says "an obvious way to improve the quality of mipmapping is to use high-quality antialiasing filters to generate the images at each mip-level" and that their 8-texel Lanczos-2 approximation "looks sharper than trilinear interpolation". Not opened. — [cardinality.pdf (not reachable)](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/schaefer/research/cardinality.pdf)

### Inferences
- (Own derivation.) In the spec model the along-axis filter is a box of length ≈ Pmax made of N bilinear taps on a 2x2-box pyramid level. A box has a sinc frequency response whose first side lobe is only about −13 dB (≈22% amplitude). Texture frequencies above the screen Nyquist along the major axis are therefore attenuated, not removed. Because filtering is linear, multiplying the level-0 spectrum by an unsharp-mask gain G(f) multiplies the aliased residue in that band by the same G(f): a +30% boost near texel Nyquist gives +30% amplitude of whatever shimmer/moiré leaks through there. Sharpening cannot create aliasing where the filter fully removes the band, and it always amplifies it where the filter leaks.
- (Own derivation.) Across the minor axis under AF, sampling is ≥ 1 screen pixel per texel of the selected level (LOD chosen from Pmax/N ≈ Pmin), so there is no minification aliasing in that direction; the texture's top octave is displayed at roughly full contrast through a bilinear tap. Sharpening is therefore fully visible in the cross-range direction, and it is bilinear reconstruction, not prefiltering, that sets the limit there.
- The trilinear path only blends two *box*-prefiltered levels. A 2-tap box per axis has response cos(pi f) (f in cycles/source texel): at 0.375 cycles/texel it still passes 38% amplitude, and that energy folds to 0.125 cycles/texel of the source (0.25 cycles/texel of the next level). So box-built mips carry baked-in aliasing of the top half-octave of the source level. If the mip chain is built *from the sharpened level 0*, that aliased component grows with the sharpening gain and is static in the texture (shows as noise/moiré in mip 1+, crawling when the mip blend changes with distance). If level 0 is sharpened *after* the chain is built from the unsharpened source, levels 1..n are unaffected.

## 2. Where is level 0 sampled in a flight-sim scene, how far out does it contribute with 16x AF, and does AF use a higher-resolution mip than isotropic filtering?

### Takeaway
Yes: per the OpenGL and Vulkan specs, AF selects the LOD from `Pmax/N` (roughly the minor axis) instead of `Pmax`, so at grazing angles it samples up to log2(16) = 4 mip levels sharper than isotropic trilinear. For a ground plane this means level 0 is in use out to a slant range of about (texel size)/(angular pixel size), independent of grazing angle while the anisotropy ratio stays ≤ 16; with 2.4 m texels that is on the order of 2.5 km at 1080p and 5 km at 4K (my derivation, assuming a 60° vertical field of view), and level 0 keeps a trilinear share out to twice that.

### Cited Findings
- **THEORY (normative spec).** OpenGL: `N = min(ceil(Pmax/Pmin), maxAniso)`, `Lamda' = log2(Pmax/N)`; without AF the LOD is `log2(Pmax)`-like. The difference is log2(N), up to 4 levels at 16x. — [EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/OpenGL-Registry/main/extensions/EXT/EXT_texture_filter_anisotropic.txt)
- **THEORY (normative spec).** Vulkan: `lambda_base = log2(rho_max/eta)` with `eta = min(rho_max/rho_min, maxAniso)`; "If eta = 1, sampling is isotropic. If eta > 1, sampling is anisotropic." — [Vulkan-Docs textures.adoc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KhronosGroup/Vulkan-Docs/main/chapters/textures.adoc)
- **CODE.** SwiftShader implements exactly this: `lod *= Rcp(anisotropy * anisotropy)` on the squared major-axis length. — [SwiftShader SamplerCore.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/swiftshader/master/src/Pipeline/SamplerCore.cpp)
- **CODE.** pbrt-v4 EWA: `lod = Levels() - 1 + Log2(shorterVecLength)`, level from the shorter axis after clamping the ratio to `maxAnisotropy`. — [pbrt-v4 mipmap.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mmp/pbrt-v4/master/src/pbrt/util/mipmap.cpp)
- **VENDOR/ENGINE DOC.** Godot documentation: "*Negative* mipmap LOD bias can also decrease performance due to higher-resolution mips having to be sampled further away." (Confirms the general principle that anything lowering the LOD pushes high-resolution levels further into the distance.) — [Godot docs, resolution_scaling.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/godotengine/godot-docs/master/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.rst)

### Inferences
All of the following is my own geometry applied to the spec formulas; no external source was found that does this for a flight simulator.

- Setup: flat ground, eye height h, slant range R to the ground point, angular pixel size θ (rad/pixel), level-0 texel size s = 2.4 m. Footprint across the view direction ≈ R·θ; along the view direction ≈ R·θ / sin(grazing angle) = R²·θ / h. Anisotropy ratio = R/h.
- Isotropic trilinear: LOD = log2(R²θ/(h·s)). Level 0 is the only level while R ≤ sqrt(h·s/θ).
- AF with ratio ≤ 16 (R ≤ 16h): LOD = log2(R·θ/s), independent of h. Level 0 is the only level while R ≤ s/θ, and has a non-zero trilinear weight until R = 2s/θ.
- AF with ratio > 16 (R > 16h, i.e. very low flight): LOD = log2(R²θ/(16·h·s)); level-0 limit R = sqrt(16·h·s/θ), 4x further than isotropic.
- Numbers for a 60° vertical FOV: 1080 lines gives θ ≈ 0.00097 rad, so s/θ ≈ 2.5 km (pure level 0) and 4.9 km (level 0 fades out). 2160 lines gives θ ≈ 0.00048 rad, so ≈ 5.0 km and 9.9 km. A narrower FOV (zoom) scales these up proportionally. Example h = 300 m at 1080p: isotropic filtering leaves level 0 at ≈ 0.86 km, 16x AF keeps it to ≈ 2.5 km (ratio there is 8.3, below the 16x clamp).
- Three zones for level 0 follow: (a) R < sqrt(h·s/θ): true magnification in both axes (bilinear upsampling; sharpening acts as pre-compensation for bilinear blur, no aliasing risk from minification). (b) sqrt(h·s/θ) < R < s/θ: about 1:1 or magnified across the view direction, minified by up to R/h along it, handled by N level-0 probes. This is the band where sharpened level 0 is both most visible and most exposed to the probe filter's leakage, and it is exactly where the ground streams fastest in screen space during low flight. (c) s/θ < R < 2s/θ: trilinear blend of level 0 and level 1.
- At cruise altitude level 0 is never touched: slant range is at least h, so if h > 2s/θ (≈ 4.9 km at 1080p/60° FOV, ≈ 9.9 km at 4K) even the nadir pixel is at LOD ≥ 1. Level-0-only sharpening therefore affects low-altitude flight, approach, taxi and take-off, and zoomed views, and has no effect on high-altitude imagery (which then comes entirely from the unsharpened levels).
- Real GPUs may deviate: the specs allow rounding N up, approximating the footprint, and reducing eta "anytime the footprint is small in texel space"; drivers also have quality/performance AF optimisations. Any implementation that under-samples the major axis (fewer probes than the ratio requires at the chosen LOD) makes zone (b) alias, and a sharpened level 0 makes that worse in proportion.

### Gaps
- X-Plane 12's actual sampler state (max anisotropy as set by the user, any global LOD bias, whether orthophoto terrain uses the same sampler as other terrain) was not researched here and is not documented in any source I could open.

### Inferences
- The documented industry positions are consistent with each other: (1) box is the safe default; (2) wider windowed-sinc filters are "better" low-pass filters but ring; (3) explicit mip sharpening exists as a shipping feature in at least one major engine (Unreal: an 8x8 kernel with negative lobes, applied while *downsampling*, i.e. to levels 1..n, not to level 0). So common practice sharpens the *minified* levels to offset box/Gaussian blur; sharpening only level 0 is the opposite of the established tool behaviour.
- A sharper downsampling filter (Kaiser/Lanczos) is not the same as "box then unsharp mask". A windowed sinc both passes more of the pass-band *and* rejects more of the stop-band (less baked-in aliasing than box). An unsharp mask after box downsampling boosts the pass-band but also boosts the aliased energy that the box let through. For temporal stability the former is the better way to get sharper mips.
- For this project the mips are built by 2x2 box in linear light; the cheapest stability-neutral improvement to distant sharpness would be a better downsampling kernel for levels 1..n rather than post-sharpening those levels. This is a design inference, not a measured result.

## 4. If only level 0 is sharpened, is there a visible contrast/sharpness discontinuity at the mip 0/1 transition? Literature on preserving contrast/variance/energy across the mip chain

### Takeaway
I found no literature that addresses sharpening only the base level; the nearest established practices go the other way (sharpen or coverage-correct the *lower* levels so they match level 0). Trilinear filtering makes the 0→1 transition a gradual blend over one octave of distance rather than a line, but a level-0-only unsharp mask adds a mid-frequency contrast step on top of the step that box-filtered mips already have, so the sharpness falloff with distance becomes steeper; whether it is visible is unmeasured. Detail-preserving downscaling papers exist (Öztireli & Gross 2015 seen as snippet only) but they target still images, and one of them explicitly criticises a predecessor for poor spatio-temporal coherence.

### Cited Findings
- **CODE/TOOL.** NVTT provides `Surface::alphaTestCoverage(alphaRef)` and `Surface::scaleAlphaToCoverage(coverage, alphaRef)`: the established "preserve a statistic across the mip chain" technique, for alpha-tested coverage, applied per mip level so lower levels match level 0. — [NVTT Surface.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/castano/nvidia-texture-tools/master/src/nvtt/Surface.cpp)
- **VENDOR DOC, `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** Unreal: mip sharpening is motivated as counteracting "the blurring the mip-mapping introduces ... to restore some of the information loss", i.e. raise contrast of levels 1..n toward level 0. — [UDK TextureProperties (not reachable)](https://docs.unrealengine.com/udk/Three/TextureProperties.html)
- **MEASURED/peer-reviewed, `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** Öztireli, A. C. & Gross, M., "Perceptually Based Downscaling of Images", ACM Transactions on Graphics 34(4) (SIGGRAPH 2015), DOI 10.1145/2766891. Per the search-returned abstract text: downscaling is formulated as an optimisation of a "widely adopted perceptual image quality metric" (SSIM) with a closed-form solution of "sums and convolutions"; the authors state results are "free of disturbing aliasing artifacts for natural images" and "spatio-temporally more coherent than methods based on kernel optimizations [Kopf et al. 2013]". Not opened; I cannot report their evaluation method (the abstract wording suggests a user study plus video results, but that is unverified). — [Author page (not reachable)](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~aco41/imageDownscaling.htm), DOI [10.1145/2766891](https://doi.org/10.1145/2766891)
- **Peer-reviewed (citation only), `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** Kopf, J., Shamir, A. & Peers, P., "Content-Adaptive Image Downscaling", ACM TOG 32(6) (SIGGRAPH Asia 2013), 173:1–173:8: confirmed only as the kernel-optimisation baseline that Öztireli & Gross describe as less spatio-temporally coherent. Abstract not retrieved. — [cited within Öztireli & Gross, DOI 10.1145/2766891](https://doi.org/10.1145/2766891)

### Inferences
All own derivations.

- Frequency view of the 0/1 step. Let f be in cycles per level-0 texel. Level 1 (2x2 box of level 0) has per-axis response cos(pi·f) relative to level 0: 0.92 at f = 0.125 and 0.71 at f = 0.25 (level 1's Nyquist). So an *unsharpened* chain already drops 8–29% contrast in the band both levels can represent, and loses everything above 0.25 cycles/texel, when going from level 0 to level 1. Trilinear filtering spreads that drop across the LOD 0→1 interval (a factor of 2 in distance).
- A Gaussian unsharp mask with amount a and radius sigma has gain G(f) = 1 + a·(1 − exp(−2·pi²·sigma²·f²)). For sigma = 1 texel: G(0.125) = 1 + 0.27a, G(0.25) = 1 + 0.71a, G(0.5) = 1 + 0.99a. For sigma = 0.5 texel: G(0.125) = 1 + 0.07a, G(0.25) = 1 + 0.27a, G(0.5) = 1 + 0.71a. With a = 0.5 and sigma = 1, level 0 gains 13–35% contrast in the band that level 1 also carries, raising the level-0/level-1 contrast ratio at level 1's Nyquist from about 1.41 to about 1.9. With sigma = 0.5 the added step is 3–13%. Small radii keep the boost mostly in the octave that only level 0 has, where there is no "mismatch" to see, only a faster fade of fine detail.
- Because the level-0 weight falls linearly with fractional LOD, the result is a smooth gradient, not a seam: sharpened texture close to the aircraft gradually loses its extra crispness over the LOD 0→1 band (≈ 2.5–5 km at 1080p per section 2). In a flight sim this band sweeps over the ground continuously, so a given ground feature loses contrast as it recedes and gains it as it approaches. That is a low-temporal-frequency contrast modulation (like normal mip behaviour, slightly stronger), not flicker.
- A level-0-only sharpen also creates a view-height dependency: the same terrain looks crisper below the altitude where level 0 is in use and reverts to unsharpened above it (section 2). Users comparing "before/after" at cruise altitude would see no change.
- Halo/overshoot: unsharp-mask overshoots at edges are part of level 0 only. Under magnification, bilinear reconstruction shows them faithfully; under the anisotropic zone they are averaged along the major axis but intact across it, so linear features crossing the view direction (roads, field boundaries, shorelines) are where edge crawl would first appear.
- If the mip chain were instead built from the sharpened level 0, the contrast step largely disappears for mid frequencies (level 1 inherits the boost), but the baked-in box-filter aliasing in levels 1..n is amplified (section 1). The two options trade a contrast gradient for extra aliasing in the distance; building levels 1..n from the unsharpened source with a better kernel avoids both.

### Gaps
- **No publication found on "sharpen level 0 only" or on perceptual visibility of a sharpness discontinuity between mip levels of an albedo texture.** I also found nothing applying Toksvig/LEAN-style variance preservation to albedo contrast; those techniques (normal-map variance to roughness) are, to my unverified recollection, about shading correctness, not about colour-texture contrast.
- Weber, N., Waechter, M., Amend, S. C., Guthe, S. & Goesele, M., "Rapid, Detail-Preserving Image Downscaling", ACM TOG 35(6) (SIGGRAPH Asia 2016): **not found by search and not opened.** No statement about it is made here.
- Whether detail-preserving downscalers are temporally stable *as mip levels under hardware filtering* is not addressed by any source I could reach; they are designed and evaluated for still-image (and, per the Öztireli snippet, video) downscaling.
- The numbers above ignore BC1 quantisation, which adds its own block-level error that sharpening may enlarge (higher local contrast is harder to fit with two endpoint colours per 4x4 block). Not researched in this note.

## 5. Is there any measured result on shimmering caused by over-sharp textures or negative LOD bias?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed measurement was found in the sources I could reach. What exists is consistent vendor guidance: NVIDIA, AMD and Godot each state in their own documentation that negative LOD bias (the closest documented analogue of an over-sharp texture) produces flickering, moiré, temporal aliasing or a "grainy in motion" look on high-frequency texture content, and each tells developers to back the bias off for such content.

### Cited Findings
- **VENDOR CLAIM.** NVIDIA, *DLSS Programming Guide*, document revision 310.6.0 (PDF dated 31 March 2026), section 3.5 "Mip-Map Bias": recommended `DlssMipLevelBias = NativeBias + log2(Render XResolution / Display XResolution) - 1.0 + epsilon`. Then, verbatim: "Due to the temporal nature of DLSS, applying the recommended negative LOD Bias to the input can sometimes lead to increased temporal stability, in the form of flickering and/or moiré. This will be highly dependent on the scene content such as texture detail. For those scenes and objects where the flickering can become distracting it will be recommended to adjust the LOD bias to be less aggressive. ... Anything less than the recommended value will tend to be softer/blurrier, so this is going to be a tradeoff between preservation of detail and aliasing." (The phrase "increased temporal stability" is evidently a typo for instability; the sentence continues "in the form of flickering and/or moiré".) — [DLSS_Programming_Guide_Release.pdf](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/DLSS/main/doc/DLSS_Programming_Guide_Release.pdf)
- **VENDOR CLAIM.** Same guide, section 3.5.1 "Mip-Map Bias Caveat: High-Frequency Textures": "If the mip levels are biased on textures with high frequency patterns, this can lead to artifacts when DLSS tries to reconstruct the full resolution frame. In particular, if trying to simulate an 'LED screen', a 'stock ticker' or something similar ... override the mip-map bias for that material and leave it at the default." — [DLSS_Programming_Guide_Release.pdf](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/DLSS/main/doc/DLSS_Programming_Guide_Release.pdf)
- **VENDOR CLAIM.** AMD, *FidelityFX Super Resolution 2* README, section "Mipmap biasing": "Applying a negative mipmap biasing will typically generate an upscaled image with better texture detail." Formula `mipBias = log2(renderResolution/displayResolution) - 1.0` (−1.58 to −2.58 for the quality modes). "It is suggested that applications adjust the MIP bias for specific high-frequency texture content which is susceptible to showing temporal aliasing issues." — [FidelityFX-FSR2 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GPUOpen-Effects/FidelityFX-FSR2/master/README.md)
- **ENGINE DOC (vendor-type claim).** Godot Engine documentation, "Resolution scaling", section "Mipmap bias": automatic negative bias "allows for better preservation of texture detail at the cost of a grainy appearance on detailed textures". Warning: "Adjusting the mipmap LOD bias manually can be useful in certain scenarios, but this should be done carefully to prevent the final image from looking grainy in motion. ... Recommended values for a manual offset are between -0.5 and 0.0." Godot adds only −0.25 for FXAA and −0.5 for TAA. Its illustrative example uses "a mipmap LOD bias of -1.0 and anisotropic filtering disabled to make the difference more noticeable". — [Godot docs, resolution_scaling.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/godotengine/godot-docs/master/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.rst)
- **OPINION (forum), `[SNIPPET ONLY]`.** NVIDIA developer forum thread "Bad texture filtering quality with negative LOD bias on Vulkan": a user reports more shimmering with negative LOD bias on Vulkan than on D3D12 with the same driver settings. User report, not opened, not verified. — [NVIDIA forum (not opened)](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/bad-texture-filtering-quality-with-negative-lod-bias-on-vulkan/195807)

### Inferences
- These statements are qualitative vendor guidance, not measurements, but three independent vendors/engines agree on direction and on the trigger ("high-frequency texture content"), and all three temporal upscalers recommend the bias *only because* a temporal accumulation pass afterwards integrates the extra aliased detail over jittered frames. A renderer without such a pass has no mechanism to absorb it. Whether X-Plane 12's anti-aliasing mode in use (MSAA and/or FXAA/TAA-type options) provides that was not researched here; MSAA does not anti-alias texture interiors at all.
- Relation to sharpening: a negative LOD bias of b moves the whole texture spectrum up by a factor 2^(−b) relative to the screen Nyquist (it under-filters). Unsharp masking leaves the band limit where it is and raises amplitude in the top band. They are different operations, but both increase energy near and above the screen Nyquist in the zones where the filter leaks, so the vendor warnings transfer in direction, not in magnitude. A mild unsharp mask (amount ≤ 0.6, small radius) is, in amplitude terms, a much smaller intervention than the −1.6…−2.6 biases those documents discuss, and Godot's "safe" manual range (−0.5…0) is likewise small. The magnitude for this use case is unmeasured.
- Practical consequence for the open question: the still-image benchmark (SSIM/PSNR/Butteraugli) cannot settle it, since the documented failure mode is temporal. A motion test is needed: e.g. render the sharpened and unsharpened textures through a software rasteriser with spec-conformant AF (SwiftShader/lavapipe) or in the sim, on a ground plane at grazing angle with sub-pixel camera translation per frame, and measure per-pixel temporal variance / flicker energy of the output after motion compensation, especially in the zone-(b) band of section 2 and on linear high-contrast features.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed, quantitative study of shimmer versus texture sharpening strength or versus LOD bias was found** in the sources reachable from this session. I could not search ACM/IEEE/arXiv full text directly, so such a study may exist; the nearest candidates I am aware of but could not open are Mavridis & Papaioannou 2011 (temporal aliasing of hardware AF, image/video evidence) and temporal-flicker quality metrics from the video-quality literature.
- No vendor document was found that discusses pre-sharpened textures as such (only LOD bias and post-process sharpening such as RCAS/NIS, which act on the final image and are outside this note's scope).
- NVIDIA/AMD driver-level "negative LOD bias: clamp" settings and their rationale were not researched (no reachable primary source).

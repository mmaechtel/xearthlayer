## 1. Classic visual-system / human-factors studies on scene content (texture density, texture type, 2D texture vs. 3D objects)

### Takeaway
The measured literature says altitude control depends on *which geometric structure* the ground texture carries (splay lines parallel to motion, depression/edge lines perpendicular to motion, vertical objects, terrain shape), not on photographic fidelity or fine-detail contrast. Texture and 3D objects act independently; for speed, the rate at which texture edges pass (edge rate) dominated judgments over global optical flow rate.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED [extract]** Flach, Hagen & Larish (1992), "Active regulation of altitude as a function of optical texture", *Perception & Psychophysics* 51(6), 557–568, DOI 10.3758/BF03211653. Three ground textures were compared: lines perpendicular to the direction of motion, lines parallel to it, and the combination (grid). Participants controlled altitude only, while disturbances were applied on vertical, lateral and fore-aft axes. "The results show a clear advantage for texture parallel to the direction of motion" (splay). The two optical sources examined were optical splay angle and global optical density / optical depression angle. — [Wright State CORE Scholar record](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/359); [Springer PDF (not reachable here)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03211653.pdf)
- **MEASURED [extract]** A 1994 conference paper by Flach, Stanard & Warren describes its results as consistent with the Flach et al. (1992) hypothesis that picking up altitude information from the flow field depends on the amount of optical activity specific to altitude change (signal) relative to other optical activity (noise). — [Wright State record "The Basis for the Perception and Control of Altitude: Splay & Depression Angle Components of Optical Flow"](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/355)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Flach, Warren, Garness, Kelly & Stanard (1997), "Perception and control of altitude: splay and depression angles", *JEP:HPP* 23(6), 1764–1782, DOI 10.1037/0096-1523.23.6.1764. Three experiments (two altitude-tracking, one "descend as low as possible without crashing"), four texture types. Texture type interacted with forward speed: performance with depression texture deteriorated with increasing forward speed; performance with splay texture was independent of forward speed; at low speeds there was little difference between the two. — [Wright State CORE Scholar record](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/333); [bepress abstract](https://works.bepress.com/john_flach/157)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Larish & Flach (1990), "Sources of optical information useful for perception of speed of rectilinear self-motion", *JEP:HPP* 16(2), 295–302. Magnitude judgments of self-speed were influenced by both edge rate and global optical flow rate; edge rate accounted for the larger share of variance; the edge-rate effect did not depend on texture type; the global-flow effect appeared to depend on whether flatness cues were controlled. — [Wright State CORE Scholar record](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/367)
- **MEASURED [extract, secondary citation]** Kleiss & Hubbard (1993), "Effects of three types of flight simulator visual scene detail on detection of altitude change", *Human Factors* 35(4), 653–671. A 1995 Air Force report cites it for two findings: texture and objects have independent effects on performance (each carries a different kind of information), and the importance of vertical objects derives from basic properties (shape/orientation) rather than realistic appearance. The primary text was not read. — [BibBase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/kleiss-hubbard-effectsofthreetypesofflightsimulatorvisualscenedetailondetectionofaltitudechange-1993); citing report [DTIC ADA303546 (not reachable here)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA303546.pdf)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Kleiss (1995), "Visual scene properties relevant for simulating low-altitude flight: a multidimensional scaling approach", *Human Factors* 37(4), 711–734. Pilots rated similarity of videotape segments and still photographs of real-world low-altitude scenes. Video results pointed to two relevant scene properties: variation in terrain shape and variation in object size or spacing. Still-photograph results were less interpretable, supporting the importance of motion information. Conclusion: simulator designers should focus on rendering terrain shape and objects. Texture was not one of the two dominant dimensions in the extract. — [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/463458); [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/en/publicatie/visual-scene-properties-relevant-simulating-low-altitude-flight-multidimensional-scaling)
- **MEASURED [extract]** A University of Toronto study, "The Use of Texture in Improving the Simulation of Low-Level Flight", found that a simple gridline texture gave superior altitude cueing in simulated low-level flight and that performance was worst with the perpendicular-line texture. Author and year were not returned in the extract. — [U of Toronto repository](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/8e311b2f-3004-419c-991e-50cc2f233590/content)
- **MEASURED [extract, fragmentary]** Buckland, Monroe & Mehrer (1980), "Flight simulator runway visual textural cues for landing", AFHRL-TR-79-81, DTIC ADA089434 (Williams AFB; Work Unit "Terrain Visual Cue Requirements for Flight Simulation"). Fragments only: additional visual cues might have reduced touchdown vertical velocities further, but the limited edge capacity of the CIG scene did not permit study of other cues; with an overrun present, pilots appeared to use overrun cues and chevron texture patterns in addition to runway texture patterns to control the landing. — [DTIC ADA089434 (not reachable here)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA089434.pdf)
- **Interpretation conflict [extract]** A commentary on the same line of work argues there is "no compelling evidence" that either parallel (splay) or perpendicular (density) texture is privileged for altitude control. Johnson, Tsang, Bennett & Phatak (1989) is cited in this literature as the study associated with lines perpendicular to the direction of travel. — [academia.edu record](https://academia.edu/1038562); [NASA NTRS record for Johnson et al.](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19880055723)

### Inferences
- The variables that moved performance in these experiments are coarse geometric ones: orientation of texture edges relative to motion, edge spacing, vertical objects, terrain relief. Unsharp masking at 2.4 m/pixel does not create new edges, splay lines or objects; it raises the amplitude of edges that already exist. On this evidence it would matter for altitude judgment only where existing fine edges are otherwise below visibility threshold.
- Because edge rate dominated speed judgments (Larish & Flach), making existing ground edges more visible could plausibly make speed cues more available at low altitude, but the count of edges per unit ground distance, which is what edge rate is, is unchanged by sharpening.
- The signal-to-noise account (Flach et al.) cuts both ways: optical activity not specific to the controlled variable acts as noise. Temporal aliasing (shimmer) produced by over-sharpened texture would be exactly such non-informative optical activity. This is an inference, not a measured result.
- The stimuli in all these studies were synthetic line, grid or dot textures and early CIG scenes. None used orthophoto texture.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Buckland et al. 1980, Kleiss & Hubbard 1993 and Kleiss 1995 could not be opened (DTIC blocked); no effect sizes or RMS-error figures were obtained for any study in this section.
- Not found or not accessible: Martin & Rinalducci (1983) "Low-level flight simulation: vertical cues"; De Maio, Rinalducci, Brooks & Brunderman (1983); Wolpert (1988, 1990); Johnson et al. (1989) results; Warren (1982); Owen & Warren (1987) (only a NASA workshop agenda naming an Owen talk surfaced: [NTRS 19920012224](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19920012224/downloads/19920012224.pdf)); Gibson, Olum & Rosenblatt (1955); Denton (1980) results (only the record: *Perception* 9(4), 393–402, DOI 10.1068/p090393, [Scilit](https://scilit.net/publications/44c5314ad194d6f9dea0b926db1dff38)).
- No study in this group varied texture *contrast* or *sharpness* as an independent variable, as far as the extracts show.

## 2. Contrast / spatial frequency and perceived speed

### Takeaway
Lower contrast makes a moving pattern look slower (the Thompson effect), and uniform contrast reduction across a driving scene lowers perceived self-speed and raises driven speed. The effect is measured for *reductions* in contrast, is attenuated at high speeds, and reverses when contrast falls with distance as in real fog. No source measured the effect of *raising* texture contrast above normal.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED [extract, secondary description]** Thompson (1982): at temporal frequencies below about 8 Hz, a low-contrast grating appears to move more slowly than a higher-contrast grating at the same physical speed. The exact title "Perceived rate of movement depends on contrast" was not confirmed by the extracts. — described in [Sotiropoulos, Seitz & Seriès, "Contrast dependency and prior expectations in human speed perception", PMC4915944](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4915944/)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Stone & Thompson (1992), "Human speed perception is contrast dependent" (NASA TM version titled "Effect of Contrast on Human Speed Perception"): with two parallel gratings moving at the same speed, the lower-contrast grating appears slower; the misperception is evident across a wide range of contrasts (2.5–50 percent); they could not replicate a switchover at 8 Hz, since in all cases lower contrast gave lower perceived speed. — [NASA NTRS 19930042953](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930042953); [NASA NTRS 19930009915](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009915)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Sotiropoulos, Seitz & Seriès (2014) report that at low contrasts the Thompson effect was attenuated at high speeds. — [PMC4915944](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4915944/)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Snowden, Stimpson & Ruddle (1998), "Speed perception fogs up as visibility drops", *Nature* 392:450. The abstract argues that drivers in fog think they are going slower than they are, which may lead them to speed up. Later sources state that fog was simulated by reducing contrast of everything in the scene equally, regardless of distance. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v392y1998i6675d10.1038_33049.html); [Cardiff ORCA record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/33877); [Culham commentary, eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/00281)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Pretto, Bresciani, Rainer & Bülthoff (2012), "Foggy perception slows us down", *eLife* 1:e00031. Psychophysics plus driving simulation: speed is underestimated when contrast is reduced uniformly for all objects independent of distance; when contrast is reduced more for distant objects, as in real fog, speed is overestimated and drivers decelerate. Average driving speed was 85 km/h in good visibility and 70 km/h in severe fog; in a distance-independent "anti-fog" condition it rose to about 104 km/h. The study used rough road pavement and roughly textured roadsides and cites earlier work that road texture is of primary importance for visual speed perception. — [eLife article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/31); [MPG press release](https://www.mpg.de/6605179/foggy-perception)
- **Commentary [extract]** Culham (2012) frames the 2012 result as a rethinking of the 1998 interpretation and notes that speed perception may depend on how contrast varies with distance from the driver. — [eLife Insight](https://elifesciences.org/articles/00281)
- **MEASURED [extract]** One study cited alongside Pretto's work used a road that was a solid black surface with no texture other than white lines and obtained different results. — [Pritchard & Hammett, Royal Holloway repository](https://repository.royalholloway.ac.uk/items/a0b5078e-90d8-d9ce-8b72-89f34b6024b5/8/Pritchard_Hammett_VR11.pdf)

### Inferences
- The direction of the effect (less contrast, lower perceived speed) makes it plausible that washed-out ground texture contributes to a weak sense of speed and that restoring local contrast moves perception toward veridical. That is an extrapolation: the measured range in Stone & Thompson was 2.5–50 percent contrast, and no extract reports perceived speed for contrast pushed above the natural level.
- Pretto et al. show that *where* in the scene contrast changes matters. Sharpening only the highest-resolution mip level raises contrast near the aircraft and leaves distant terrain unchanged, which is a near-high/far-low contrast gradient, the same sign as fog. In their data that gradient produced speed *over*estimation. Whether a mild mip-level sharpening produces a measurable effect of this kind is untested.
- Attenuation of the Thompson effect at high speeds suggests any contrast-driven change in perceived speed would be largest for slow image motion (high altitude, low angular velocity) and smallest near the ground.

### Gaps
- No effect size was obtained for Thompson (1982) or Snowden et al. (1998); full texts were not reachable.
- Not found: Diener et al. (1976) on spatial frequency and perceived velocity; Distler & Bülthoff (1996); Owens, Wood & Carberry (2010). No finding on spatial-frequency content and perceived speed can be reported from these.
- No study found on supra-normal (enhanced) contrast or on edge-enhanced imagery and perceived self-speed.

## 3. Resolution / detail requirements, standards, and scintillation / aliasing as a negative cue

### Takeaway
The regulation text that could be confirmed specifies resolution in angular terms (surface resolution of 2 arc minutes), not texture contrast or sharpness. Image-generator patents treat scintillation, crawling and other aliasing artifacts as misleading visual information that the system must avoid; no measured study of shimmer as a distractor was found.

### Cited Findings
- **STANDARD/REGULATION [extract]** 14 CFR Part 60: surface resolution tolerance "not greater than two (2) arc minutes", demonstrated with white runway markings on a black runway surface viewed from a 3° glide slope at slant ranges where the marking subtends 2 arc minutes; a lower level requires not greater than three (3) arc minutes. A separate requirement gives a minimum resolution of 5 arc-minutes for computed and displayed pixel size. Day-scene runway markings are required "as required by the surface resolution requirements". — [eCFR Part 60](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-60); [Cornell LII Appendix A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/appendix-A_to_part_60); [Cornell LII Appendix C](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/appendix-C_to_part_60)
- **STANDARD/REGULATION [extract]** The 9 May 2008 final rule (Amdt. 60-3) clarified and standardised requirements for visual scene content, with appendices reflecting international standards; the 30 March 2016 rule (Amdt. 60-4) aligned Part 60 with ICAO 9625 and added visual tests including field of view and lightpoint brightness. — [Federal Register 2008-05-09](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-05-09/pdf/08-1183.pdf); [Federal Register 2016-03-30](https://docs.regulations.justia.com/entries/2016-03-30/2016-05860.pdf)
- **VENDOR CLAIM (patent text) [extract]** A U.S. patent on a modular digital image generator states the imagery should be free from stair-stepping, crawling, line breakup and scintillation so the trainee is not given misleading visual information ("free from anomalies or aliasing effects"). Another image-generator patent calls aliasing unacceptable because it severely degrades realism. — [US 4,570,233](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4570233); [US 4,715,005](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4715005)
- **Reference fact [extract]** The first patent on mipmap and texture generation was issued in 1983 to Yan, Szabo and Chen of Link Flight Simulation (Singer). Prefiltered texture levels were introduced in the flight-simulator industry. — [Wikipedia, Mipmap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mipmap)
- **Review, existence only [extract]** Padmos & Milders (1992), "Quality criteria for simulator images: a literature review", *Human Factors*: gives criteria for about 30 outside-world image features of CGI systems on vehicle simulators, with an introduction on visual perception and per-feature discussion. A June 1991 TNO report by Milders & Padmos ("Requirements for outside-world images of vehicle simulators; a literature survey") covers flight, driving and ship simulators. The specific criteria were not visible. — [Taylor & Francis chapter record](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315243092-6/quality-criteria-simulator-images-literature-review-pieter-padmos-maarten-milders); [DTIC ADA257726 (not reachable here)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA257726.pdf)
- **MEASURED [extract]** NASA Langley synthetic-vision terrain-portrayal studies (head-down displays, not out-the-window visuals): elevation-based generic texture significantly improved perceived situation awareness without adverse effect on flight technical performance or workload; pilots performed similarly with generic and photo-realistic texture although most preferred photo-realistic; one report suggests the extra detail of photo-realistic texture slowed the pilot's scan of airspeed, altitude and heading. — [NTRS 20040034179](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040034179/downloads/20040034179.pdf); [NTRS 20030014490](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20030014490/downloads/20030014490.pdf); [NASA/TP-2003-212177](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20030051605/downloads/20030051605.pdf)

### Inferences
- A requirement of 2 arc minutes surface resolution tested with white-on-black markings is a statement about resolvable high-contrast detail. A 2.4 m ground sample subtends 2 arc minutes at roughly 4.1 km slant range (2.4 m / tan(2′)); below that range the orthophoto, not the display, limits detail, which is the regime where sharpening could change what is seen. This arithmetic is mine, not from a source.
- The industry position in the patents is that temporal aliasing is a false cue, to be removed by prefiltering. Unsharp masking the top mip level works in the opposite direction to prefiltering: it adds energy near the texel Nyquist frequency, which is the energy that aliases when the texture is minified between mip levels or viewed obliquely.
- The NASA display results are counter-evidence to "more photographic detail is better", but they concern a head-down display and must not be read as out-the-window results.

### Gaps
- **Unverified recollection, not confirmed in this session:** I recall Part 60 visual-scene tables containing wording that scenes must provide sufficient surface and textural cues to assess sink rate and depth perception during landing and be free of apparent or distracting quantization (aliasing). Two targeted searches did not return this text and the regulation pages could not be opened. Treat as a lead to check in Appendix A, Attachment 3 (Tables A3B/A3C), not as a cited fact.
- ICAO Doc 9625 and EASA CS-FSTD(A) wording: not accessed.
- AGARD-AR-159, AGARD-AR-164 and Statler (1981): not found or accessed.
- No NASA/AFRL guidance on "texture resolution versus altitude" for photo-specific databases was found.
- No measured study of scintillation or shimmer as a distraction or negative training cue was found; the only statements are patent and practitioner assertions.

## 4. Geo-specific imagery practice: sharpening and contrast processing of ortho imagery, mipmaps, detail textures

### Takeaway
Ortho4XP has a real, optional per-provider filter chain including a `sharpness` filter (Pillow `ImageEnhance.Sharpness`), verified in its source; it is off by default. X-Plane's own approach to missing close-range detail is not sharpening but greyscale detail textures ("decals") that modulate the albedo at a much higher repeat frequency. AutoOrtho's repository contains no sharpening code.

### Cited Findings
- **SOURCE CODE (read directly)** Ortho4XP `src/O4_Imagery_Utils.py`, function `color_transform(im, color_code)`: supported filters are `brightness-contrast` (GIMP formula, range −127…127), `saturation` (`ImageEnhance.Color`), `sharpness` (`ImageEnhance.Sharpness(im).enhance(value)`), `blur` (`ImageFilter.GaussianBlur(radius)`) and `levels` (per-channel in_min, gamma, in_max, out_min, out_max). — [Ortho4XP source](https://github.com/oscarpilote/Ortho4XP/blob/master/src/O4_Imagery_Utils.py)
- **SOURCE CODE (read directly)** Filters are read from `*.flt` files in the `Filters` directory (one filter per line, name then numeric arguments) and are bound to an imagery provider by a `color_filters=` key in the provider's `.lay` file; providers without the key default to `"none"`. The transform is applied to the assembled orthophoto before texture conversion only when `color_filters != "none"`. — [Ortho4XP source](https://github.com/oscarpilote/Ortho4XP/blob/master/src/O4_Imagery_Utils.py)
- **SOURCE CODE (read directly)** In the repository, `color_filters` appears only in a handful of provider files (Belgium GeoPunt2012, Netherlands PDOK variants); in `Providers/Netherland/PDOK.lay` the line is commented out (`#color_filters=PDOK20`). The shipped `Filters/GeoPunt2012.flt` contains only `levels` and `brightness-contrast -25 10`, no sharpness. — [PDOK.lay](https://github.com/oscarpilote/Ortho4XP/blob/master/Providers/Netherland/PDOK.lay); [GeoPunt2012.flt](https://github.com/oscarpilote/Ortho4XP/blob/master/Filters/GeoPunt2012.flt)
- **SOURCE CODE (GitHub code search)** Searches of the AutoOrtho repository for "sharpen", "sharpness", "unsharp" and "contrast" each returned zero hits. — [kubilus1/autoortho](https://github.com/kubilus1/autoortho)
- **FORUM/BLOG OPINION [extract]** x-plane.org thread "how to make images sharper" (Ortho4XP forum, 2018): a user describes adding an unsharp/sharpness filter through the Ortho4XP colour-correction configuration, set per imagery source; the thread lists the available filters as brightness-contrast, saturation, sharpness, blur and levels. The post refers to an older config key (`full_color_correction`), so details may not match current versions. — [x-plane.org forum](https://forums.x-plane.org/forums/topic/138806-how-to-make-images-sharper/?tab=comments)
- **VENDOR CLAIM (tool author) [extract]** OrthoStreamXP release notes state that each new texture is sharpened a little as it is built. No rationale or evaluation was visible. — [x-plane.org file page](https://forums.x-plane.org/files/file/102530-orthostreamxp-free-satellite-aerial-ortho-streaming-for-x-plane-12/)
- **FORUM/BLOG OPINION [extract]** A FlyAway Simulation guide says driver-level sharpening "can also exaggerate halos and distant shimmer", and that anisotropic filtering sharpens textures viewed at shallow angles. A related page explains that without mipmaps a distant marking covering few screen pixels but many texels makes details appear and disappear as the camera moves. — [FlyAway Simulation, X-Plane 12 graphics](https://flyawaysimulation.com/ask/answers/improve-graphics-quality-x-plane-12/); [FlyAway Simulation, mipmaps](https://flyawaysimulation.com/ask/answers/anti-aliasing-mipmapping-flight-simulators/)
- **VENDOR CLAIM (Laminar Research) [extract]** "Using decals to add detail to scenery": decals are overlay textures layered over the daytime albedo to add high-frequency, high-resolution detail (examples: grassy patterns on fields, gravel on runways); they only modulate the underlying texture; the basic `DECAL` takes a greyscale texture applied evenly across the albedo, with an example at 25× the albedo frequency, repeating every 60 m; decals are allowed in `.ter`, `.pol` and `.net` files. — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/article/using-decals-to-add-detail-to-scenery/)
- **VENDOR CLAIM (Laminar Research) [extract]** Laminar later calls decals "Detail Textures" (DECAL is the legacy term), designed to add higher-density textural detail without resorting to large texel densities; X-Plane 12.1 added normal-map decals that add high-frequency detail by changing surface normals rather than colour. 12.1 is described as primarily a graphics release including RCAS (a post-process sharpening pass) and new decals. — [developer.x-plane.com ?p=40926](https://developer.x-plane.com/?p=40926); [developer.x-plane.com ?p=40841](https://developer.x-plane.com/?p=40841); [developer.x-plane.com news](https://developer.x-plane.com/category/news/)

### Inferences
- Sharpening ortho imagery is an established but optional practitioner habit, not a default: the principal generator ships the capability switched off, and the only tool found that sharpens by default does so "a little".
- Laminar's design choice addresses the same complaint (ground looks soft at low altitude) by adding a separate high-frequency layer that repeats at metre scale, i.e. it adds detail *above* the orthophoto's resolution limit rather than amplifying detail at that limit. This supplies new texture edges (relevant to edge rate, section 1) whereas unsharp masking does not.
- If Ortho4XP-built packages were already sharpened by their author, sharpening again at runtime would compound; whether a given package was built with a sharpness filter cannot be told from the package without the build configuration.
- X-Plane 12.1's RCAS pass already sharpens the final image in screen space; a texture-space unsharp mask would stack with it.

### Gaps
- developer.x-plane.com could not be opened; the decal statements are from extracts, and no Laminar text explaining a rationale for decals *specifically over orthophotos* was found.
- The meaning of the Pillow `Sharpness` factor (1.0 = unchanged, above 1 sharpens) is from general knowledge of Pillow and was not checked against its documentation in this session.
- No documentation from CAE, Collins/Evans & Sutherland, Presagis, Diamond Visionics, Aechelon, VT MAK, Asobo/Microsoft or Lockheed Martin (Prepar3D) on sharpening or contrast-processing geo-specific imagery was found. No I/ITSEC or IMAGE Society paper on the subject was retrieved.
- No credible, specific experience report (forum or otherwise) was found that describes shimmer caused by sharpened ortho tiles in X-Plane; the only shimmer statement concerns driver-level sharpening in general.

## 5. Driving simulation: texture / contrast and speed perception or production

### Takeaway
Driving-simulator reviews list image contrast among the factors affecting speed perception, and the one experiment with numbers (Pretto et al. 2012) shows driven speed changing by roughly −18% to +22% across contrast manipulations. Nothing found manipulated road-surface texture sharpness.

### Cited Findings
- **Review [extract, via citing papers]** Kemeny & Panerai (2003), "Evaluating perception in driving simulation experiments", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, DOI 10.1016/S1364-6613(02)00011-6: re-examines visuo-vestibular interaction and the transfer of simulator results to the real world. Citing papers summarise its list of factors influencing speed perception as optic flow, time-to-contact, field of view, angular declination, image contrast and weather conditions. — [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/en/publicatie/evaluating-perception-driving-simulation-experiments); [Colombet et al., ENSAM repository](https://sam.ensam.eu/bitstream/handle/10985/6782/Colombet%20JCISE%20Special%20Issue%20Driving%20Simulation%202010.pdf)
- **MEASURED [extract, secondary]** A citing paper attributes to Jamson (2000) the finding that image resolution and field of view have a significant impact on speed perception in driving simulators. — [Colombet et al., ENSAM repository](https://sam.ensam.eu/bitstream/handle/10985/6782/Colombet%20JCISE%20Special%20Issue%20Driving%20Simulation%202010.pdf)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Pretto et al. (2012): 85 km/h (good visibility), 70 km/h (severe distance-dependent fog), about 104 km/h (distance-independent contrast reduction). — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/31)
- **Thesis, existence only [extract]** Pretto's doctoral thesis, "The Perception and Production of Speed During Self-Motion: Evidence for Non-Optimal Compensation Mechanisms", covers perceived and produced speed under reduced image contrast, with chapters "Fog decreases the produced speed" and "Fog decreases the driving speed also in a realistic driving scenario". — [University of Padova repository](https://www.research.unipd.it/bitstream/11577/3425122/1/PrettoP_Thesis_PhD.pdf)

### Inferences
- From the Pretto figures: 70/85 is about an 18% reduction in driven speed and 104/85 about a 22% increase. These percentages are my arithmetic on the reported means; they describe strong visibility manipulations, far larger than a mild unsharp mask.
- Image resolution being a significant factor (Jamson, secondary) is the closest driving-simulation evidence that visible fine detail affects speed perception, but the direction and size were not available.

### Gaps
- No Pretto & Chatziastros (2006) paper was located; Chatziastros appears only in the thesis acknowledgements.
- Not found: Mourant et al. (2007) on optic flow and geometric field of view; Hurwitz, Knodler & Dulaski; Denton's stripe results.
- Kemeny & Panerai's own text on contrast was not read; the factor list is from citing papers.

## 6. Counter-evidence: added detail that did not help, or hurt

### Takeaway
Training-transfer work by Lintern and colleagues contains findings in both directions, including one where a low-detail scene trained landing better than a moderate-detail one. Together with Kleiss (1995) and the NASA display studies, the evidence does not support "more surface detail is reliably better".

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED [extract]** Lintern and co-authors (1997), "Transfer and quasi-transfer effects of scene detail and visual augmentation in landing training": training with a low-detail scene was better for transfer than training with a moderate-detail scene; augmented guidance enhanced transfer with the low-detail scene but degraded it with the moderate-detail scene. — [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib1990/1987/)
- **MEASURED [extract], conflicting** A 1991 SAE paper by overlapping authors, "Simulator scene detail and visual augmentation guidance in landing training for beginning pilots" (SAE 912099): transfer assessed in a criterion simulator configuration showed advantages for more training trials, visual augmented guidance, and *moderate* scene detail. — [SAE Mobilus 912099](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/simulator-scene-detail-visual-augmentation-guidance-landing-training-beginning-pilots-912099)
- **Bibliographic only** Lintern & Koonce (1992), "Visual augmentation and scene detail effects in flight training", *International Journal of Aviation Psychology* 2(4), 281–301. No abstract was available. — [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/567400)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Kleiss (1995): terrain shape and object size/spacing, not surface texture, emerged as the scene properties pilots treated as relevant for low-altitude flight. — [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/463458)
- **MEASURED [extract]** Flach et al. (1997): at low forward speeds texture type made little difference to altitude control. — [Wright State CORE Scholar](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/333)
- **MEASURED [extract]** NASA synthetic-vision flight test: similar performance with generic and photo-realistic terrain texture despite a preference for photo-realistic. — [NTRS 20040034179](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040034179/downloads/20040034179.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pilot preference and measured performance diverge in at least one study (NASA): a sharper-looking ground may be preferred without changing altitude or speed control. A subjective "looks better" verdict on sharpening is therefore not evidence of better cueing.
- The Lintern results concern training transfer for novices, a different question from an experienced desktop-sim user's in-flight perception, and the two Lintern-group results disagree on the sign of the scene-detail effect.

### Gaps
- The definitions of "low" and "moderate" scene detail in the Lintern studies were not available; they are unlikely to correspond to texture sharpness.
- No study was found reporting that higher texture contrast or sharpness *impaired* altitude or speed judgment; the negative case for sharpening rests on aliasing reasoning and practitioner statements, not on measurement.

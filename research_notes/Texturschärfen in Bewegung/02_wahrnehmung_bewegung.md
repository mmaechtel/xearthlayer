## 1. Spatiotemporal / spatiovelocity contrast sensitivity: loss of high-spatial-frequency sensitivity with retinal velocity

### Takeaway
All sources agree qualitatively: as *retinal* velocity rises, the contrast-sensitivity curve slides towards lower spatial frequencies and the high-frequency cutoff (acuity) falls quickly, while peak sensitivity stays roughly constant up to very high speeds. The decisive variable is retinal velocity (image velocity minus eye velocity), not screen velocity. The exact Kelly/Daly formula constants could not be verified in this session.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED.** Kelly, D. H. (1979), "Motion and vision. II. Stabilized spatio-temporal threshold surface", JOSA 69(10), 1340-1349, DOI 10.1364/JOSA.69.001340. Abstract: the constant-velocity passband shifts across the spatio-temporal frequency domain and stays nearly symmetric about the constant-velocity plane at 2 deg/s; at low velocities (range of natural eye drift) the stabilized CSF matches the normal unstabilized one. Measured with retinally stabilized images, so velocity means retinal velocity. — [Kelly 1979, JOSA (abstract via DOI)](https://www.doi.org/10.1364/JOSA.69.001340)
- **MODEL (secondary description of Kelly).** At constant retinal velocity the stabilized CSF is tuned to a spatial frequency inversely related to the velocity; the spatiotemporal CSF can equivalently be written as a function of spatial frequency and retinal velocity. — [castleCSF, Ashraf et al. 2024, J. Vision](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2793530)
- **MODEL (secondary).** Above a retinal velocity of 0.15 deg/s, peak sensitivity drops and the whole curve shifts to the left (lower spatial frequencies); the CSF is "translated toward lower spatial frequencies and acuity drop[s] fairly rapidly as velocity increases". — [Yee et al., "Spatiotemporal sensitivity and visual attention for efficient rendering of dynamic environments" (arXiv cs/0511032), as excerpted by search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0511032)
- **MODEL (patent, secondary).** A perception-based rendering patent approximates spatial acuity vs. retinal velocity from Kelly's data with a corner velocity of about 2 deg/s (acuity is roughly constant below, falls above). — [US patent 6,028,608](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6028608)
- **MEASURED.** Burr, D. C. & Ross, J. (1982), "Contrast sensitivity at high velocities", Vision Research 22(4), 479-484, DOI 10.1016/0042-6989(82)90196-1. Abstract: the spatial frequency needing least contrast for detection falls as grating speed rises, but peak sensitivity stays the same at speeds up to 800 deg/s. — [Burr & Ross 1982 (Univ. Florence record)](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/680139)
- **MODEL.** Daly, S. J. (1998), "Engineering observations from spatiovelocity and spatiotemporal visual models", Proc. SPIE 3299, DOI 10.1117/12.320110. Extends Kelly's spatiovelocity CSF with drift, smooth pursuit and saccades; models the envelope of spatial sensitivity as a function of retinal velocity. — [Daly 1998, SPIE](https://opticalengineering.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/3299/0000/Engineering-observations-from-spatiovelocity-and-spatiotemporal-visual-models/10.1117/12.320110.full)
- **MODEL (secondary).** A Daly-based spatiovelocity CSF peaks at about 5 deg/s in velocity. — [CIC 15 paper "Measurement and Modeling of Viewing-Condition-Dependent ..." (IS&T), as excerpted by search](https://library.imaging.org/admin/apis/public/api/ist/website/downloadArticle/cic/15/1/art00020)
- **MEASURED + MODEL.** Laird, J., Rosen, M., Pelz, J., Montag, E. & Daly, S. (2006), "Spatio-velocity CSF as a function of retinal velocity using unstabilized stimuli", Proc. SPIE 6057 (Human Vision and Electronic Imaging XI), DOI 10.1117/12.647870. Eye-tracked psychophysics with Gabor patterns at three spatial and three temporal frequencies: sensitivity was determined by the retinal velocity produced by the Gabors, regardless of the type of eye motion; Kelly/Daly model parameters were re-tuned to the data. — [RIT repository record](https://repository.rit.edu/other/171); [SPIE record](https://www.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/6057/1/Spatio-velocity-CSF-as-a-function-of-retinal-velocity-using/10.1117/12.647870.short?SSO=1)
- **MODEL.** Mantiuk, R. K., Ashraf, M. & Chapiro, A. (2022), "stelaCSF: a unified model of contrast sensitivity as the function of spatio-temporal frequency, eccentricity, luminance and area", ACM TOG (SIGGRAPH 2022). Fitted to data from 11 papers covering a 5-dimensional space; earlier models covered static or foveal content, not both. Code is public. — [project page](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/research/rainbow/projects/stelaCSF/); [code](https://github.com/gfxdisp/stelaCSF)
- **MODEL.** Ashraf, M., Mantiuk, R. K., Chapiro, A. & Wuerger, S. (2024), "castleCSF — A contrast sensitivity function of color, area, spatiotemporal frequency, luminance and eccentricity", Journal of Vision 24(4):5, DOI 10.1167/jov.24.4.5. Fitted to 18 datasets with a prediction error of 3.59 dB. — [J. Vision](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2793530)
- **MEASURED (secondary summary, weak).** Robson, J. G. (1966), "Spatial and temporal contrast-sensitivity functions of the visual system", JOSA 56, 1141, DOI 10.1364/josa.56.001141. A database summary states that at high spatial frequencies the surround's influence is negligible and sensitivity is set mainly by the centre's temporal characteristics. — [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/spatial-and-temporal-contrast-sensitivity-functions-of-the-visual-system/811893890054356995-20628)

### Inferences
- Temporal frequency of a drifting pattern is `velocity x spatial frequency`. A fixed temporal limit therefore removes high spatial frequencies first as retinal velocity grows; that is the mechanism behind the leftward shift in every source above. (Derivation, not a cited number.)
- Because Laird et al. found retinal velocity to be the governing variable independent of eye-movement type, the question "is fine texture detail visible in motion?" reduces to "what is the retinal velocity of that texture patch?" — i.e. to question 2.
- Burr & Ross's constant peak sensitivity means motion does not lower contrast sensitivity overall; it relocates it. Coarse structure remains fully visible at speeds where texel-scale detail is not.
- stelaCSF/castleCSF are the practical tools if the project wants a numerical answer: evaluate sensitivity at (spatial frequency of texel-scale detail in cpd, temporal frequency = retinal velocity x that frequency). I could not run or read them here.

### Gaps
- **Kelly/Daly formula constants not verified.** I recall Daly's acuity-limit term as `k_max = 45.9 / (v + 2)` cpd (v = retinal velocity in deg/s), which would give roughly 23 cpd at 0, 15 cpd at 1, 6.6 cpd at 5, 3.8 cpd at 10 and 1.5 cpd at 30 deg/s. **This is from memory, not from any source opened in this session**; one targeted search failed to surface the equation. The independent patent statement of a ~2 deg/s corner velocity is consistent with it but is not confirmation. Verify against Daly (1998) before quoting.
- Sources conflict on where the shift begins: 0.15 deg/s (Yee et al. excerpt) vs. ~2 deg/s corner velocity (patent on Kelly's data). Probably different quantities (onset of peak drop vs. acuity corner), but unresolved without full texts.
- No quantitative values extracted from stelaCSF or castleCSF for high spatial frequency at given temporal frequencies (PDFs unreachable).
- Robson (1966): original abstract not obtained; the claim above is a third-party summary and concerns mechanism, not a numeric cutoff.
- None of these data are for grazing-angle, perspective-compressed natural texture; all are gratings/Gabors, mostly foveal.

## 2. Smooth pursuit, retinal velocity of tracked vs. untracked texture, optic flow in flight

### Takeaway
Pursuit is good but imperfect: gain is about 0.9-1.0 below 20 deg/s and Daly's engineering model assumes 82% tracking up to a ceiling of 80 deg/s, so even tracked texture keeps a residual retinal slip of roughly 18% of its screen speed. In a flow field only the fixated/tracked point is stabilized; everything else moves on the retina, so most of the ground texture is seen at non-zero retinal velocity.

### Cited Findings
- **MODEL (secondary, quoting Daly 1998).** Daly's eye-movement heuristic: compensated retinal velocity = image velocity minus a tracking term built from 0.82 x image velocity, bounded below by 0.15 deg/s (drift of a fixating eye) and above by 80 deg/s (highest speed the eye tracks efficiently). The 0.82 comes from Daly's data fit, i.e. roughly 82% tracking efficiency. — [Yee et al., arXiv cs/0511032 (search excerpt)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0511032)
- **MEASURED/MODEL (secondary, thesis reproducing Daly).** The eye tracks targets reliably up to about 80 deg/s; beyond that tracking is erratic. — [Univ. Lübeck dissertation (search excerpt)](https://www.zhb.uni-luebeck.de/epubs/ediss812.pdf)
- **MEASURED (review statement).** Pursuit gain (eye velocity / target velocity) is normally 0.9-1.0 for target velocities below 20 deg/s. — [review of pursuit models, PMC2603166 (search excerpt)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2603166)
- **MEASURED (search excerpt, source not individually identified).** One study reports linear pursuit behaviour up to 75 deg/s with gain about 0.9 under certain conditions; another that gain decreases monotonically as target velocity increases. — [Springer, DOI 10.1007/BF00236858](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00236858)
- **Citation only.** Meyer, C. H., Lasker, A. G. & Robinson, D. A. (1985), "The upper limit of human smooth pursuit velocity", Vision Research 25(4), 561-563, DOI 10.1016/0042-6989(85)90160-9. Existence confirmed; abstract and numbers not retrieved. — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/8e0b8b93c644e759c750f6d1ed6d7e1a)
- **MEASURED.** Laird et al. (2006): contrast sensitivity follows retinal velocity whatever the eye movement type (see section 1). — [RIT repository](https://repository.rit.edu/other/171)
- **MEASURED/analysis.** Matthis et al., "Retinal optic flow during natural locomotion", PLOS Computational Biology: gaze stabilization during fixation nulls motion at the fovea, so retinal flow always consists of outflowing motion centred on the fixation point. — [PLOS Comput Biol](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1009575)
- **MEASURED (preprint, provisional).** In a walking study, looking at the ground consisted of a series of brief fixations and small saccades keeping gaze a roughly fixed distance ahead, rather than continuous smooth pursuit. — ["The Role of Retinal Flow in Walking", bioRxiv / J. Vision](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2818649)
- **MEASURED.** Drivers' gaze is consistent with tracking waypoints on the future path 1-3 s ahead. — [Tuhkanen et al. 2019, "Humans Use Predictive Gaze Strategies to Target Waypoints for Steering", Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-44723-0)

### Inferences
- With Daly's 0.82 factor, a tracked texture patch moving at screen speed v leaves about 0.18 v on the retina (never below 0.15 deg/s): about 1.8 deg/s at 10 deg/s, 5.4 deg/s at 30 deg/s. Untracked texture has retinal velocity equal to its screen velocity relative to gaze. (Arithmetic on the cited model.)
- In a flight view the flow speed varies strongly across the screen (slow near the horizon/far field, fast in the near field at the bottom of the screen). A viewer fixating the horizon, an instrument or a distant landmark sees the near-field texture at close to full screen velocity; a viewer who tracks a ground feature stabilizes only a small neighbourhood around it. So the high-resolution mip level, which is used nearest the camera where angular flow is fastest, is the content most often seen at high retinal velocity. This is my reasoning from the cited flow geometry, not a cited measurement.
- The far field at grazing angles moves slowly on screen, so there retinal velocity is low and still-image sharpness judgements transfer best; but that region is also minified and drawn from lower mip levels, so sharpening of the top level contributes little there.

### Gaps
- No source found for gaze behaviour of pilots or flight-sim users over ground texture, nor measured retinal slip in flight optic flow. The locomotion/driving literature is an analogy.
- Meyer et al. (1985) numbers not obtained. The figure sometimes quoted in the brief (pursuit limits "30-80 deg/s") is supported here only at the 80 deg/s end (Daly, via secondary sources) and by the <20 deg/s gain statement.
- Daly's 0.82/0.15/80 constants are from secondary sources quoting Daly, not from Daly's text.

## 3. Dynamic visual acuity (DVA)

### Takeaway
Acuity for a moving target that the observer tries to track deteriorates with angular velocity, slowly at first and then steeply (cubic law): about 2 arcmin at 20 deg/s, about 3 arcmin at 50 deg/s, 6-7 arcmin at 110 deg/s in Ludvigh & Miller's data. The loss is attributed to retinal slip from imperfect pursuit.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED + MODEL.** Ludvigh, E. & Miller, J. W. (1958), "Study of Visual Acuity during the Ocular Pursuit of Moving Test Objects. I. Introduction", JOSA 48(11), 799. Acuity drops markedly as angular velocity rises; data fit the semi-empirical law `Y = a + b x^3` (Y = threshold in arcmin, a = static acuity, x = angular velocity in deg/s, b = dynamic-acuity factor). — [JOSA abstract](https://opg.optica.org/josa/abstract.cfm?uri=josa-48-11-799)
- **MEASURED.** Companion paper (Part II, direction of movement, relative movement, illumination), JOSA 48(11), 803: mean thresholds over nine subjects, horizontal and vertical movement, about 1.95 arcmin at 20 deg/s, 2.84 arcmin at 50 deg/s and 6.63 arcmin at 110 deg/s. Part I also lists group values of 2.36, 3.57 and 2.02 arcmin for three performance groups at 20/50 deg/s (table only partly visible in the search excerpt). — [JOSA abstract Part II](https://opg.optica.org/josa/abstract.cfm?uri=josa-48-11-803)
- **MEASURED (secondary).** In a different setup acuity fell to about 20/200 at 100 deg/s. — ["Dynamic visual acuity using 'far' and 'near' targets" (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7912721_Dynamic_visual_acuity_using_far''_and_near''_targets)
- **Review statements.** The term DVA was introduced by Ludvigh & Miller (1958); Brown (1972) is cited for foveal DVA diminishing with stimulus angular velocity; eye-tracking studies (incl. Demer & Amjadi 1993, Sloan optotypes, 16 s exposure) point to inadequate pursuit as the key cause. Studies disagree on the speed at which DVA starts to deteriorate markedly because methods differ. — [NAP "Emergent Techniques for Assessment of Visual Performance", ch. 5](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/916/chapter/5); [Quevedo et al., "Dynamic Visual Acuity", SciELO](https://www.scielo.br/j/tpsy/a/YwsvdhnBtQgM35TmqpLV7BH/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- 1.95 arcmin at 20 deg/s corresponds to roughly half the resolution of a 1 arcmin (20/20) static observer; 6.6 arcmin at 110 deg/s to roughly one sixth. (Arithmetic on the cited thresholds; static baseline of the subjects not retrieved.)
- DVA is measured with a single high-contrast optotype the observer is told to track — the best case. Streaming ground texture that is not being tracked will be resolved worse than DVA figures suggest.
- The cubic law implies that at low screen speeds (a few deg/s) tracked detail is essentially as sharp as static detail; sharpening benefit should survive slow drift under pursuit.

### Gaps
- Brown (1972) and Demer & Amjadi (1993) were not opened; only review-level descriptions are available. No numbers from them.
- No DVA data for low-contrast natural texture.

## 4. "Motion sharpening"

### Takeaway
Blurred edges and gratings look sharper when they move (or are shown briefly) than when static; the effect grows with speed and can reach about 50% for flashed edges, while already-sharp edges tend to look more blurred. It is a change in appearance, not in resolving power — blur discrimination does not improve — so motion tends to *compress* perceived sharpness differences between sharp and soft versions.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED.** Ramachandran, V. S., Madhusudhan Rao, V. & Vidyasagar, T. R. (1974), "Sharpness constancy during movement perception", Perception 3(1), 97-98. Short note; cited by later work as an early report that moving blurred images may look sharper than the same images stationary. — [cited in Hammett, Georgeson & Barbieri-Hesse 2003, Perception, DOI 10.1068/p3400](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3400)
- **MEASURED.** Bex, P. J., Edgar, G. K. & Smith, A. T. (1995), "Sharpening of drifting, blurred images", Vision Research 35(18), 2539-2546. Sine gratings and blurred edges: perceived blur was inversely related to drift speed and contrast; moving blurred images may appear sharper than static ones. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/004269899500060D)
- **MEASURED.** Hammett, S. T. & Bex, P. J. (1996), "Motion sharpening: evidence for the addition of high spatial frequencies to the effective neural image", Vision Research 36(17), 2729-2733. Motion sharpening at the test frequency was smaller after adaptation to missing-fundamental patterns; proposes an early non-linearity that adds higher spatial frequencies to the neural image. — [Royal Holloway portal](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/motion-sharpening-evidence-for-the-addition-of-high-spatial-frequ/)
- **MEASURED + MODEL.** Hammett, S. T., Georgeson, M. A. & Gorea, A. (1998), "Motion blur and motion sharpening: temporal smear and local contrast non-linearity", Vision Research 38(14), 2099-2108. Both motion blur and motion sharpening occurred, depending on the physical blur of the pattern. Model: temporal integration smears moving images, and a local contrast non-linearity increasingly sharpens the effective edge profile as speed and contrast increase (possibly M vs. P cell recruitment). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0042698997004306)
- **MEASURED.** Hammett (1997), "Motion blur and motion sharpening in the human visual system", Vision Research: when perceived blur was held constant, blur discrimination was roughly constant for speeds up to at least 6.3 deg/s (as described by a later paper). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004269899700059X)
- **MEASURED.** Georgeson, M. A. & Hammett, S. T. (2002), "Seeing blur: 'motion sharpening' without motion", Proc. R. Soc. B 269(1499), 1429-1434, DOI 10.1098/rspb.2002.2029. Blurred edges look up to 50% sharper when presented briefly (8-24 ms) than at 100-500 ms, without motion; this argues against explanations that require motion or compensate for motion blur. — [Aston research portal](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/seeing-blur-motion-sharpening-without-motion/)
- **MEASURED + MODEL.** Hammett, Georgeson & Barbieri-Hesse (2003), "Motion, flash, and flicker: a unified spatiotemporal model of perceived edge sharpening", Perception, DOI 10.1068/p3400: the degree of non-linearity must increase with speed or temporal frequency. Related 2003 Vision Research paper ("A model for motion sharpening: contrast gain control precedes compressive nonlinearity"): sharpening increases with speed but is practically invariant with contrast. — [Perception](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3400); [Aston portal](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/a-model-for-motion-sharpening-contrast-gain-control-precedes-comp/)
- **MEASURED.** Burr, D. (1980), "Motion smear", Nature 284, 164-165. A moving target exposed long enough to be seen clearly as motion shows far less smear than expected. Reviews report perceived smear of moving dots grows with duration up to about 40 ms and shrinks for longer durations; the effect holds mainly for dense dot arrays, not isolated targets. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/284164a0.pdf); [review, PMC2864981](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864981)
- **MEASURED + OPINION.** Burr, D. C. & Morgan, M. J. (1997), "Motion deblurring in human vision", Proc. R. Soc. B 264(1380), 431-436, DOI 10.1098/rspb.1997.0061. Moving objects look more blurred in brief than in long exposures, yet deblurring does not improve discrimination; authors' conclusion: moving objects appear sharp "not because of some special mechanism that removes blur, but because the visual system is unable to perform the discrimination necessary to decide whether the moving object is really sharp or not". — [PMC1688277 (abstract via search)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1688277)
- **MEASURED.** Takeuchi, T. & De Valois, K. K. (2005), "Sharpening image motion based on the spatio-temporal characteristics of human vision", Proc. SPIE 5666 (HVEI X), 83-94, DOI 10.1117/12.586425. Natural-image movies: strongest motion sharpening when frames were spatially band-reject filtered; spatial low-pass gave more sharpening than high-pass; temporal low-pass filtering increased perceived sharpness, temporal high-pass drastically reduced perceived motion quality; perceived contrast of high-spatial-frequency components in moving images is enhanced by interaction between spatio-temporal channels. Authors' conclusion (OPINION): moving images can be compressed by removing higher spatio-temporal frequencies. — [SPIE](https://www.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/5666/1/Sharpening-image-motion-based-on-the-spatio-temporal-characteristics-of/10.1117/12.586425.short)

### Inferences
- Conflict worth keeping: Bex et al. (1995) report perceived blur decreasing with contrast as well as speed; Hammett/Georgeson (2003) report sharpening nearly invariant with contrast. Different stimuli and models; not resolvable from abstracts.
- Hammett et al. (1998) "both blur and sharpening depending on physical blur" plus Westerink & Teunissen (section 5) point the same way: in motion, soft content is perceived as sharper and sharp content as slightly softer, i.e. the perceived difference between an unsharpened and an unsharp-masked texture is likely smaller in motion than in a still A/B comparison.
- Burr & Morgan's discrimination result suggests observers in motion are poor at telling whether an edge is "really sharp" — which would apply equally to telling whether it carries extra sharpening.
- Takeuchi & De Valois imply the visual system itself supplies apparent high-frequency contrast in motion, which reduces the marginal perceptual return of adding real high-frequency energy.

### Gaps
- Blur widths (arcmin), exact speed ranges and effect sizes for Bex 1995, Hammett & Bex 1996 and Hammett et al. 1998 could not be extracted (abstracts only). The only hard effect size obtained is "up to 50%" (Georgeson & Hammett 2002, flashed edges) and "6.3 deg/s" (Hammett 1997).
- Ramachandran et al. (1974) was not opened; known only via citation.
- All motion-sharpening studies cited use isolated edges/gratings or a few movies; none uses perspective ground texture.

## 5. Visibility of overshoot / ringing / halos in moving vs. static images; perceived sharpness of moving images; contrast constancy

### Takeaway
**I found no psychophysical or video-quality study that directly measures the visibility of unsharp-mask overshoot/halos as a function of image motion.** What exists is (a) measured perceived sharpness of moving natural images, showing that motion compresses sharpness differences and that fixation vs. pursuit matters, and (b) patent/vendor lore on halo control that is not motion-specific.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED.** Westerink, J. H. D. M. & Teunissen, C. (1990), "Perceived sharpness in moving images", Proc. SPIE 1249, DOI 10.1117/12.19662. A portrait moving horizontally at 0.5-35 deg/s on a high-resolution monitor, sharpness rated on a categorical scale: for maximum-resolution images hardly any change in perceived sharpness with velocity; for low-resolution images sharpness rises with velocity (authors read this as compression of the perceived sharpness range). — [SPIE](https://nanolithography.spiedigitallibrary.org/conference-proceedings-of-spie/1249/0000/Perceived-sharpness-in-moving-images/10.1117/12.19662.full); [TU Eindhoven record](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/perceived-sharpness-in-moving-images/)
- **MEASURED.** Westerink, J. H. D. M. & Teunissen, K. (1995), "Perceived sharpness in complex moving images", Displays 16(2), 89-97, DOI 10.1016/0141-9382(95)91178-5. Fixation condition (image velocities up to 5.3 deg/s): sharpness decreases with velocity, especially for maximum-resolution images. Pursuit condition (object velocities up to 35 deg/s): only a small decrease for maximum-resolution images and a considerable increase in sharpness for lower-resolution images. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0141938295911785); [abstract index](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/28987485)
- **MEASURED.** Georgeson, M. A. & Sullivan, G. D. (1975), "Contrast constancy: deblurring in human vision by spatial frequency channels", J. Physiology 252, 627-656. Suprathreshold contrast matching across spatial frequencies is performed correctly (especially above 5 c/deg) despite large differences in thresholds; well above threshold, perceived contrast is roughly independent of spatial frequency. — [PMC1348487 (abstract via search)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1348487)
- **OPINION (vendor page, not a study).** Overshoot can produce a visible halo; perceived quality improves when overshoot is narrower and lower, but removing it entirely makes natural images look flat; there is an optimum overshoot width/height per edge strength. Not motion-specific. — [VOCAL, "Overshoot for Video Sharpening"](https://vocal.com/video/overshoot-for-video-sharpening/)
- **OPINION (patents, not studies).** A small controlled overshoot adds to apparent sharpness without being perceived as a halo; halos become objectionable when overshoot is large and extends well beyond a high-contrast edge. Not motion-specific. — [US 8,488,063](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8488063); [US 10,846,829](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10846829)

### Inferences
- Is contrast constancy relevant? Yes, as a caution against over-reading the CSF: the spatiovelocity CSF describes *detection thresholds*. Unsharp-mask overshoot on high-contrast edges is usually far above threshold, and Georgeson & Sullivan show suprathreshold perceived contrast does not follow the threshold curve. So "the CSF says high frequencies are invisible at v deg/s" does not by itself prove that a strong halo is invisible; it only bounds faint detail. Whether contrast constancy holds across *temporal* frequency/velocity was not established by anything I could access.
- Halo geometry argument (my reasoning): an unsharp-mask halo has a spatial scale set by the mask radius, i.e. it is lower in spatial frequency than the texel-scale detail it enhances. Since motion removes the highest spatial frequencies first (section 1), a wide-radius halo should survive motion better than the fine detail whose sharpness was the point of the sharpening. That would make large-radius/high-amount settings relatively *less* favourable in motion than in stills, and small-radius settings more favourable. This is an inference, not a measured result.
- Westerink & Teunissen's fixation result (sharpness of maximum-resolution content falls with velocity already below 5.3 deg/s) is the closest measured evidence that extra top-level sharpness is partly lost on untracked moving content; their pursuit result says it is largely retained on tracked content up to 35 deg/s.

### Gaps
- **No solid study found** on halo/overshoot/ringing visibility in moving vs. static images, on "motion-adaptive sharpening" evaluated psychophysically, or on SID/JSID picture-enhancement studies addressing this. Two searches returned only patents and vendor pages. Teunissen/Heynderickx work on sharpness enhancement was not located.
- Two statements surfaced in search summaries without a recoverable source and are therefore **not** used as findings: (i) that in a Westerink & Teunissen experiment average eye velocities were 25% and 65% of stimulus velocity at higher and lower speeds; (ii) that "artificial sharpening of edges in blurred moving images does not necessarily enhance the perceptual quality" (possibly from a J. Vision/VSS abstract "Perceived sharpness of moving natural images", [link returned by search](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2131170), which a follow-up search could not confirm). Both are highly relevant and worth checking by hand.

## 6. Temporal masking, flicker and temporal aliasing (shimmer)

### Takeaway
No study was found that weighs shimmer from sharpened, minified texture against the sharpness benefit. The available evidence is indirect: aliasing-induced flicker is a recognized artefact to which the visual system is sensitive, including in the periphery, where sensitivity to fine static detail is poor.

### Cited Findings
- **OPINION/teaching material.** When texels shrink below pixel size, small camera or object motion makes pixel colours jump, producing "shimmering" or "buzzing", classed as an aliasing problem. — [UCSD CSE168 lecture notes, Rotenberg](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/sp17/cse168-a/CSE168_06_Antialiasing.pdf)
- **MEASURED.** In a subjective 2AFC study, TAA was significantly better at suppressing peripheral flicker than FXAA and SMAA, which scored close to no anti-aliasing. — ["Perceptual Visibility Model for Temporal Contrast Changes in Periphery", arXiv 2205.00108](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00108)
- **Review statement.** The visual system is sensitive to temporal aliasing artefacts even at higher eccentricities. — ["Foveated Rendering: Motivation, Taxonomy, and Research Directions", arXiv 2205.04529](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.04529)
- **Review statement.** Flicker visibility decreases with increasing refresh rate; rendering-induced temporal and spatial aliasing is an artefact that is rarely considered in display-quality models. — [Univ. Cambridge technical report UCAM-CL-TR-977](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-977.pdf)
- **MODEL.** stelaCSF benchmarked on flicker data: spatial RMSE about 6.13 dB, CFF RMSE 11.47 (as reported by a later flicker model paper), i.e. general CSF models predict flicker thresholds less well than spatial thresholds. — [elaTCSF, arXiv 2503.16759](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16759)

### Inferences
- Sharpening the top mip level raises its high-frequency energy. Wherever that level is sampled under minification without sufficient filtering (grazing angles, anisotropic limits, mip bias), the added energy aliases into temporal flicker at low spatial frequency on screen. Low-spatial-frequency flicker sits near the peak of temporal sensitivity (section 1: peak sensitivity is preserved in motion; only the preferred spatial frequency shifts down), whereas the intended sharpness gain sits at high spatial frequencies where motion lowers sensitivity. So in motion the artefact can be more visible than the benefit even when the still image looks better. This is an inference from sections 1 and 6, not a measured result.
- This risk depends on how X-Plane builds and samples mips: if lower mip levels are generated from the *sharpened* top level, the boost propagates; if they are pre-generated (as in XEarthLayer's own 5-level chain) from unsharpened data, only the top level is affected and the risk is confined to where level 0 is actually minified. The latter depends on implementation facts outside this research scope.

### Gaps
- No psychophysical study found comparing shimmer visibility with sharpness benefit, nor any measuring flicker sensitivity specifically for aliased high-spatial-frequency texture under sharpening.
- No source obtained on temporal/motion masking of spatial artefacts (e.g. whether motion masks halos). The classical video-coding claim that motion masks spatial detail was not verified here.
- Robson (1966) and Kelly (1979) numeric flicker sensitivities at high spatial frequency not extracted.

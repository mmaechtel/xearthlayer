**Contrast and perceived speed**

- **MEASURED [ABSTRACT OPENED + NASA TM full text OPENED]** Stone, L. S. & Thompson, P. (1992), "Human speed perception is contrast dependent", *Vision Research* (NTRS 19930042953); NASA-TM-103898 "Effect of contrast on human speed perception" (NTRS 19930009915). Decisive sentences: "When two parallel gratings moving at the same speed are presented simultaneously, the lower-contrast grating appears slower. This misperception is evident across a wide range of contrasts (2.5-50 percent) and does not appear to saturate (e.g. a 50 percent contrast grating appears slower than a 70 percent contrast grating moving at the same speed). On average, a 70 percent contrast grating must be slowed by 35 percent to match a 10 percent contrast grating moving at 2 deg/sec (N = 6). Furthermore, the effect is largely independent of the absolute contrast level and is a quasi-linear function of log contrast ratio. ... although spatial frequency has little effect, relative orientation is important. Finally, the misperception of relative speed appears lessened when the stimuli to be matched are presented sequentially." ### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The NASA records confirm the round-1 reading that this literature manipulates *geometry* of texture (orientation, spacing, density, flow rate), never contrast or sharpness. They also show the geometry question itself is unsettled (Johnson: parallel texture worst; Flach as reported in round 1: parallel texture best; Wolpert: flow rate, not edge rate, drives altitude control).
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Stone & Thompson's "does not appear to saturate" (tested up to 70 % vs 50 %) removes the easy argument that orthophoto contrast is already "high enough" for the Thompson effect to be irrelevant, but only for simultaneously presented parallel gratings at 2 deg/s; see 2b for the opposing evidence.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Pretto's uniform-reduction result did not differ between moderate (RMS 0.31) and severe (RMS 0.19) reduction in perceived speed, although driven speed did (93.6 vs 101.3 km/h). So even in the best data set the dose-response of global contrast on perceived self-speed is not monotone in a simple way.

## 2a. Counter-evidence to "geometry matters, fine detail does not": cases where fine texture or resolution DID matter

### Takeaway
There is real counter-evidence, mostly from NASA Ames helicopter simulation: pilots could not judge ground speed and altitude "owing to the lack of fine texture", and good runway texture improved sink-rate control and descent-rate perception. But the same laboratory measured that the *finest* texture was *not* the best, because it aliased in motion. The evidence says fine detail near the ground is functionally useful when it is real, resolved detail; none of it concerns amplifying the contrast of detail that is already at the resolution limit.

### Cited Findings
- **OPINION of test pilots, reported in a NASA experience report [OPENED]** Condon, G. W. (1991), "Simulation of nap-of-the-Earth flight in helicopters", NASA-TM-102830, NTRS 19910011818. Dash/quick-stop task, simulator vs flight: "In flight, they relied on the external scene to judge the vehicle's altitude and ground speed ... In the simulation, they relied more on aircraft instruments ... They gave two reasons for this change in strategy: first, they could not judge ground speed and altitude from the CGI scene owing to the lack of fine texture; and second, the restricted field of view limited altitude information during pitch changes." Conclusion 6 of the report: "Accurate ground-speed and height sensing, which are crucial to nap-of-the-Earth flight, require fine scene texture." Conclusion 5: "The addition of easily recognizable scaling objects contributes greatly to the pilot's ability to estimate range and range rate." Also: half-size checkerboard squares in the last quarter of the landing zone "provided the pilots a finer gradation for height control"; pilots said "reduced resolution and lack of depth perception ... detracted from doing precision maneuvering". These are pilot comments and handling-quality comparisons, not controlled texture experiments. — [NTRS 19910011818](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19910011818)
- **MEASURED [ABSTRACT OPENED]** Schroeder, J. A., Dearing, M. G., Sweet, B. T., Kaiser, M. K., et al. (2001), "Runway Texture and Grid Pattern Effects on Rate-of-Descent Perception" (AIAA 2001-4307), NTRS 20010048652. Context: "Pilots tend to touch down twice as hard in simulation than in flight". Four textures (incl. none) x three grid spacings (incl. none), steep approaches: "The results showed that pilot better controlled their vertical descent rates when good texture cues were present. No significant differences were found for the grid manipulation." A follow-up psychophysical staircase tested descent-rate discrimination with the same scenes (the NTRS abstract is truncated before the result). — [NTRS 20010048652](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20010048652)
- **MEASURED, reported in a review [OPENED]** Aponso, B. L., Tran, D. T., Schroeder, J. A. (2009), "Rotorcraft Research at the NASA Vertical Motion Simulator", NTRS 20100024324. On the autorotation study (Dearing et al. 2001): "Visual texture affected all measures, but the finest texture did not perform the best, debunking a myth that more texture is better. This result was also supported by a fixed-base psychophysics study (Ref. 52). Visual detail affected only pilot subjective opinion." On Johnson's level-of-detail study: "changing the level-of-detail to maintain constant optical density as the altitude changed, like that of the real world, improved altitude awareness." On a further study varying "spatial frequency with alternating black-and-white stripes": "The variations in visual scene evaluated had no effect, while the motion configurations did have an effect." — [NTRS 20100024324](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20100024324)
- **MEASURED [ABSTRACT OPENED]** Johnson, W. W., Schroeder, J., Statler, I. C. (1994), "Visual-Motion Cueing in Altitude and Yaw Control", NTRS 20020011020 (VMS, simulated AH-64): "both constant optical density and platform motion improved altitude awareness in an altitude repositioning task"; finding 4: "LOD management yielding constant optical density across altitudes improves altitude awareness." — [NTRS 20020011020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20020011020)
- **Expert review [OPENED]** Sweet, B. T. & Kaiser, M. K. (2011), "Depth Perception, Cueing, and Control", AIAA 2011-6424, NTRS 20180007277: "Computer generated scenes do not typically provide correct texture gradient cues. ... Photorealistic textures are commonly used, often to provide realistic ground detail. ... a naturally-viewed texture will typically have features at eye-limiting resolutions at all locations in the image. Although an observer can typically expect to see more fine detail in the closest surfaces and objects in a naturally viewed scene, computer generated scenes frequently do not provide this characteristics. Practical limits on the amount of texture information available in a computer database limit the amount of scene detail that can be shown, and this is most visible at the closest approach to surfaces. Methods to improve the near-field scene detail include mapping additional layers of finer texture (i.e., level-of-detail rendering) and implementing textures that are computed in real time to increase level of detail." And: "Veridical texture mapping, particularly in the near-field ... is still not easily achieved (if at all)". Also: "Photorealistic textures (from satellite or aerial photography) often have shadows cast by trees, clouds, and buildings that are a part of the photograph. This can cause a non-realistic shadow". — [NTRS 20180007277](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20180007277)
- **Expert consensus [OPENED]** Statler (ed.) 1981 (AGARD WG-10): "Whereas the high-flying pilot can obtain adequate height cues from size and shape of known objects, the low-flying pilot must make use of texture and parallax." "Pilot ratings and performance in the low-speed and hover flight regimes are extremely sensitive to the available visual and motion cues"; for a failure to hover the FSAA simulator with a camera/model-board visual, possible explanations "could have been an inadequate resolution or dead bands in the camera drive or possibly even deficiencies in the motion system." On range effects: "the familiar reduction in visibility with increasing range is a common natural phenomenon. However, a reduction in the apparent fine detail associated with close objects is not". The difference in luminance-transition density between a real runway scene and its simulation "is primarily due to the runway texture, which in the case of the simulated scene was either absent from the model or not acquired by the visual simulation system." — [NTRS 19810012531](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19810012531)
- **MEASURED [OPENED]** Wolpert 1991 (above): in active altitude holding, global optical flow rate, not edge rate, drove altitude errors (3.4 % vs 0.8 % of variance). — [NTRS 19920012232](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920012232)

### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The round-1 conclusion needs softening, not reversal. It should read: altitude/speed judgment depends on optical structure (splay, flow rate, edge rate, optical density) **and** the visual system needs enough resolvable texture elements near the ground to carry that structure; simulators that lacked fine texture measurably and reportedly degraded height and ground-speed judgment. What is *not* supported anywhere is that raising the contrast of existing texture elements, without adding elements, improves these judgments.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The constant-optical-density result (Johnson/Schroeder/Statler) is the most transferable principle: as the aircraft descends, new finer detail must appear so that the number of texture elements per visual angle stays roughly constant. A 2.4 m/pixel orthophoto cannot do that below the altitude at which a texel becomes larger than a display pixel; unsharp masking does not change element count, whereas X-Plane's detail textures (section 3) or higher-zoom imagery do. Sweet & Kaiser name exactly "additional layers of finer texture" as the remedy.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** "Visual detail affected only pilot subjective opinion" (VMS review) plus the NASA synthetic-vision preference/performance split means a "looks sharper, feels faster/lower" impression from sharpening would not be evidence of better control.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** All positive fine-texture evidence comes from hover, nap-of-the-earth, autorotation and landing flare, i.e. eye heights of a few metres to tens of metres. At those heights a 2.4 m texel covers degrees of visual angle; sharpening a 2.4 m sampled image cannot supply the centimetre-to-decimetre detail those studies are about.

### Gaps
- Dearing et al. 2001 (AIAA Modeling and Simulation Technologies Conference, Montreal) itself was not opened; its result is taken from two NASA papers by its own co-authors (Aponso/Tran/Schroeder 2009; Sweet et al. 2014). No effect sizes are available.
- The truncated NTRS abstract of Schroeder et al. 2001 does not state the psychophysical outcome.
- Martin & Rinalducci 1983, De Maio et al. 1983, Kleiss & Hubbard (texture effect) and Buckland et al. 1980 could not be checked (DTIC). They remain plausible further counter-evidence that I cannot quote.
- No study found that varies texture *contrast* (as opposed to presence, density, resolution or pattern) in an altitude- or speed-control task. The Statler report notes generally that "Little objective data exist on precisely what is required".

## 2b. Counter-evidence to "more near-field contrast could cause speed overestimation": saturation and reversal of the contrast-speed effect

### Takeaway
The evidence is mixed and does not support a confident prediction in either direction. For simultaneously shown parallel gratings the effect does not saturate up to 70 % contrast (Stone & Thompson); with orthogonal gratings, with sequential presentation and at high speeds it weakens, saturates or (in some studies) reverses. The only driving-scene study shows that the *spatial distribution* of contrast matters, with near-good/far-poor contrast producing overestimation, but it tested large reductions, not a mild near-field increase.

### Cited Findings
- **MEASURED [OPENED]** Stone & Thompson 1992 (NASA-TM-103898): for parallel gratings the effect "does not appear to saturate"; with orthogonal gratings "The effect showed greater intersubject variability, evidence of saturation, and dependence on absolute contrast"; "Saturation is suggested by the fact that not one of the three subjects showed a significant difference in the perceived speed of a 70% test and a 50% standard when tested with orthogonal gratings"; and the effect "appears lessened when the stimuli to be matched are presented sequentially." Spatial frequency "has little effect". — [NTRS 19930009915](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009915)
- **MEASURED [OPENED]** Sotiropoulos, Seitz & Seriès (2014), PMC4915944 (6 subjects, 5 days, drifting gratings, 2 cycles/deg, speeds up to 12 deg/s, contrasts 3 % to 95 % Michelson):
  - Literature summary by the authors: "there is some evidence that the Thompson effect is attenuated or even reversed at higher speeds"; "Thompson (1982) found that this ratio was indeed less than 1 for temporal frequencies below 8 Hz"; "a small number of studies have presented evidence for the opposite effect: in certain cases, low contrast results in an increase in perceived speed."
  - Their own result: "In all other cases, CDB was less than one, meaning that lowering contrast resulted in a decrease in perceived speed. In other words, apart from the aforementioned single case, there was no 'null point' – a result that conflicts with that of Thompson, Brooks, and Hammett (2006)."
  - Interaction: "at the lowest tested contrasts, CDB decreases as speed increases ...: lowering contrast does not decrease perceived speed as much when speeds are high. Interestingly, the situation is reversed at the highest tested contrasts (15% and 95% Michelson contrast) and CDB becomes more prominent at high speeds."
  - On why studies disagree: reference contrasts differed (25 %, 64 %, 70 %), and "when subjects are highly uncertain about the relative speed of two gratings, they may be inclined to pick the grating of the higher contrast as being the faster one (because it is also the most salient)."
  — [PMC4915944](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4915944/)
- **MEASURED [OPENED]** Pretto et al. 2012: uniform contrast reduction to RMS 0.31 and to RMS 0.19 produced the *same* perceived-speed bias (41.4 vs 41.5 km/h equivalent; "did not differ significantly from one another"), i.e. the perceptual effect was saturated between these two levels. Speed discrimination was worse (higher JND) under uniform reduction (0.58 km/h) than under clear or fog (0.41, 0.40, 0.42 km/h). Overestimation occurred only when contrast was better near than far (fog); the opposite gradient (anti-fog) gave underestimation (19 km/h equivalent at 60 km/h) and doubled JND (0.543 vs 0.248/0.225 km/h). — [PMC3479833](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479833/)
- **MEASURED, reported in a review [OPENED]** Sweet, Stone, Liston & Hebert (2014/IMAGE 2008), NTRS 20140013240: "Li, Sweet, and Stone (2005, 2006) conducted several experiments that demonstrated the effect of visual motion perception on manual control tasks dependent on velocity perception. Performance was degraded when visual motion perception was compromised, both through contrast and color variations." And, citing Lindholm, Sharine & Pierce (2003): "They report that studies on perceived speed during low-level flight had produced inconsistent results." — [NTRS 20140013240](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140013240)

### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The round-1 fog analogy ("sharpening the top mip level creates a near-high/far-low contrast gradient, the sign that produced overestimation") is logically consistent with Pretto's mechanism but is an extrapolation across a large gap: Pretto removed most contrast from the far field (RMS 0.46 to 0.19-0.31), whereas unsharp masking adds a modest amount of high-spatial-frequency contrast to the near field and leaves low-frequency (global RMS) contrast almost unchanged. Stone & Thompson found spatial frequency to have "little effect" on the bias and the effect to scale with log contrast *ratio*; a mild sharpening changes the contrast ratio between near and far only slightly. Expected size of any speed bias: small. Sign: toward faster if anything. Not measured anywhere.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Counter-evidence against the worry is real: weaker or absent effect with non-parallel patterns, with sequential comparison (which is the situation of a pilot comparing "now" with memory), and inconsistent results in low-level flight. Counter-evidence against dismissing it is also real: no saturation up to 70 % for parallel gratings, and at high contrasts Sotiropoulos et al. found the contrast effect *more* prominent at high speeds.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The statement "restoring contrast moves perceived speed toward veridical" (round 1) is supported in direction by Stone & Thompson's terrain remark ("over low-contrast terrain like water or sand") and by Li/Sweet/Stone (control performance degraded when motion perception was compromised through contrast), but only for genuinely low-contrast imagery.

### Gaps
- Thompson, Brooks & Hammett 2006 ("Speed can go up as well as down at low contrast") was **not** opened (PubMed blocked); it is known here only through Sotiropoulos et al., who failed to replicate its null point.
- Owens, Wood & Carberry 2010, Horswill & Plooy 2008, Snowden et al. 1998, Blakemore & Snowden 1999, Li, Sweet & Stone 2005/2006 and Lindholm et al. 2003 were not opened; they appear only as cited by the opened papers.
- No study on supra-normal, edge-enhanced or sharpened imagery and perceived self-speed was found.

## 2c. Is scintillation / aliasing a recognised negative cue? Statements at source

### Takeaway
Yes, at the level of expert consensus, and with one measured performance result. The AGARD WG-10 report states that texture in motion "can cause severe scintillation, especially if the contrast is not greatly attenuated", that such anomalies "can impose incorrect perceptions of speed" and may cause negative transfer, while also stating that their effects "have not been quantified" and that pilots habituate if they are "not excessive". A NASA Ames study found the highest-resolution ground texture gave worse sink-rate control because of spatio-temporal aliasing.

### Cited Findings
- **STANDARD / expert consensus [OPENED]** Statler (ed.) 1981, NASA-TM-81278 (AGARD FMP WG-10):
  - "Texturing, under conditions of motion, can cause severe scintillation, especially if the contrast is not greatly attenuated. Proper correction of this scintillation through anti-aliasing hardware is very expensive. One possibility for minimizing the scintillation problem without major investments in image generation hardware is to use color contrast to introduce the texturing rather than luminance contrast."
  - "Flight instructors and pilots undergoing training have some very descriptive names for these visual anomalies: shearing, tearing, flickering, creeping, sparkling, streaking, bouncing, scintillating, racing, jumping, skipping, edge walking, and reversing ... The potential effects of these visual phenomena have not been quantified. They are at least annoying and create distractions that may interfere with pilot performance by causing frequent short-interval delays thereby increasing the pilot's workload. In addition to being distracting, they can impose incorrect perceptions of speed when they take the form of slowdowns or movement reversals. They may provide navigational and spatial orientation cues that do not exist in the real world and thereby lead to negative transfer of training."
  - Tolerance (counter-statement in the same report): "Another class of visual stimuli, usually described as distracting effects, comprises those stimuli that are due to aliasing or to the spatial quantization of the image by a CGI visual system. ... If these effects are not excessive, pilots become accustomed to them and the effects have little or no bearing on most training tasks." The report then speculates that habituation might degrade target-acquisition skills and adds: "These ideas are purely speculative but warrant further investigation."
  - Concrete case: a runway centreline of 150-ft painted segments interacted with the raster so that during approach "the centerline of the runway suddenly begins to slow down, stops, then reverses its direction"; fixed by restructuring the model. Forecast: "We may not have such success in the future if the scenes become more detailed or if we add texture to the scene in mathematically repeatable patterns. If the scene content becomes cyclic, nonuniform perceptions of speed will occur as stroboscopic effects, phi phenomenon, or flickering." "There is no current systematic evaluation being made of the temporal-spatial perceptions induced by any of the existing visual systems."
  - On the design goal of anti-aliasing: improved techniques "result in images that have high subjective resolution and sharpness, with the higher spatial frequencies appropriately attenuated."
  - On contrast enhancement (the one passage that speaks *for* it): "Resolution and contrast are interrelated. Any optical system operating near its limit of resolution will suffer a decrease in contrast; the sharpness of the picture will decrease. ... Artificial enhancement of contrast in the image-generation equipment may overcome the effect of some of the noise sources." and "contrast has a much greater effect on usable resolution" than luminance.
  — [NTRS 19810012531](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19810012531)
- **MEASURED, reported by a co-author in a review [OPENED]** Sweet, B. T., Stone, L. S., Liston, D. B., Hebert, T. M., "Effects of Spatio-Temporal Aliasing on Out-the-Window Visual Systems" (presented IMAGE 2008; NTRS 20140013240): "Dearing, Schroeder, Sweet, and Kaiser (2001) ... One of the hypotheses prior to the study was that if the ground texture resolution was too high, that spatio-temporal aliasing would result in impaired motion perception and reduced performance. Three texture levels were chosen, the highest-resolution being chosen (subjectively) based on the level of aliasing observed during the final portion of the approach. The highest resolution texture was associated with worse performance, in sink rate, than the other textures which did not create significant spatio-temporal aliasing. At the highest rate, the combination of vehicle motion, proximity to the textured ground plane, and texture resolution combined to create a perception of flashing or incoherent motion. The two lower texture resolutions appeared to move relatively smoothly and consistently." — [NTRS 20140013240](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140013240)
- **Analysis, reported in the same review [OPENED]** "Lindholm, Sharine, and Pierce (2003) ... analyzed the degree to which spatio-temporal aliasing would occur in realistic flight simulation applications, with both high-resolution (0.15 m/pixel) and low-resolution (0.56 m/pixel) textures, at simulated altitudes of 500 and 50 m, as a function of ground speed (100 to 800 knots). They demonstrated that at a majority of these conditions, a large proportion of a simulated ground plane would exhibit spatio-temporal aliasing, on the order of 80 to 90% of the ground plane for the lowest altitude and highest resolution texture." — [NTRS 20140013240](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140013240)
- **Review statements [OPENED]** Same paper: without filtering or mip-mapping "the resulting perceived texture can appear to be have a much lower spatial frequency, or the periodicity that might originally be present in a texture could appear to be highly irregular or incoherent"; "When target motion is under-sampled, humans perceive artifacts that disrupt acuity and distort spatial features of the target (e.g. doubling, blurring), and ability of smooth pursuit to continuously track moving objects is degraded." Remedies: "There are two potential solutions for reducing the saliency of spatio-temporal aliasing; 1) increasing refresh rate, and/or 2) decreasing the spatial resolution (or low-pass filtering) of the image." "Current mip-mapping techniques could possibly be modified to include image velocity in selecting the spatial resolution of textures". Empirical refresh-rate results quoted: motion artifacts reported at 60 Hz in all conditions (10-40 deg/s), at 120 Hz only at 40 deg/s, none at 240 Hz (Winterbottom et al. 2008, 11.5 cycles/deg projector). Limits of the evidence, in the authors' words: "very little research has demonstrated a direct correlation between spatio-temporal aliasing and simulator performance"; "objective measures of the impact of this artifact do not currently exist"; sampled-motion artifacts are less evident in pursuit than in fixation, and "refresh rates of 60 Hz are routinely used with acceptable image performance." — [NTRS 20140013240](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140013240)

### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** "Scintillation is a recognised negative cue" stands as a consensus statement and has one supporting measurement (Dearing et al.), but the same sources say the effect is unquantified, that moderate aliasing is habituated to, and that 60 Hz systems are routinely accepted. Honest wording: recognised as a risk, demonstrated once, not quantified.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The AGARD sentence ties scintillation directly to **contrast** of moving texture ("especially if the contrast is not greatly attenuated"). Unsharp masking raises exactly the luminance contrast of the highest spatial frequencies in the texture, i.e. it acts opposite to the two remedies Sweet et al. name (low-pass filtering, lower spatial resolution).
- **OWN INFERENCE (arithmetic).** The temporal frequency of a ground pattern of wavelength L passing at ground speed v is v/L, independent of altitude and viewing angle. The finest component a 2.4 m/pixel texture can hold has L = 4.8 m (texel Nyquist). At 60 frames/s the frame-rate Nyquist limit is 30 Hz, reached at v = 30 x 4.8 = 144 m/s (about 280 kt); at 30 frames/s at 72 m/s (about 140 kt). Below those speeds the top-level texture content is temporally well sampled; above them the components that sharpening boosts most are the ones that alias in time. For comparison, Lindholm's textures were 0.15 and 0.56 m/pixel (Nyquist wavelengths 0.3 and 1.12 m), which reach 30 Hz at 9 m/s and 34 m/s; this is why they found aliasing nearly everywhere and why a 2.4 m orthophoto is far less exposed. Real orthophotos are not periodic, so the artifact would be judder/flashing of fine edges rather than motion reversal.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Spatial aliasing (shimmer) is a separate mechanism: it arises where the top mip level is shown at slightly less than one texel per pixel or at oblique angles under limited anisotropic filtering. If only mip level 0 is sharpened and the lower levels are built from the unsharpened image, the transition band between level 0 and level 1 becomes a visible sharpness step that moves with the aircraft. Untested; listed as a design risk.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The AGARD remark that artificial contrast enhancement "may overcome the effect of some of the noise sources" near the resolution limit is the closest thing in this literature to an endorsement of sharpening: it justifies compensating a known MTF loss (e.g. blur from resampling/JPEG/BC1), not boosting beyond the original scene contrast.

### Gaps
- FAA AC 120-40B, 14 CFR Part 60, ICAO 9625 and CS-FSTD(A) texts were not reachable; none of the opened NASA reports quotes their wording on "distracting quantization". The round-1 recollection about Part 60 remains unverified.
- AGARD-AR-159 not found. The AGARD-numbered print of AR-164 not opened (DTIC); only the NASA edition was read.
- Dearing et al. 2001, Lindholm et al. 2003 (I/ITSEC), Winterbottom et al. 2008, Kuroki et al. 2006, Watson, Ahumada & Farrell 1986 were not opened; quoted as reported by Sweet et al.
- No measured threshold exists for how much texture contrast produces objectionable scintillation.

## 2d. Aviation evidence on image sharpness/resolution of sensor or display imagery and pilot performance

### Takeaway
Very little could be opened. The usable statements are qualitative: limited display resolution degrades motion information and precision manoeuvring; display resolution limits achievable depth cues; more photographic detail on a head-down display did not improve and may have degraded flight technical performance. No sharpening study (NVG, FLIR, EVS) was found on the reachable hosts.

### Cited Findings
- **OPINION of test pilots [OPENED]** Condon 1991 (NASA-TM-102830): "The pilots commented that the reduced resolution and lack of depth perception ... in the simulator detracted from doing precision maneuvering in the simulator." — [NTRS 19910011818](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19910011818)
- **Expert review [OPENED]** Sweet & Kaiser 2011: helmet-mounted displays "can achieve resolutions in the range of 1.6 to 4.0 amin/pix", which limits the achievable stereo disparity threshold. — [NTRS 20180007277](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20180007277)
- **MEASURED [OPENED]** Hughes & Glaab 2003 (head-down SVS): photo-realistic texture plus high-resolution DEM "may combine to detract from optimal pilot performance (due to the extreme level of detail ...)", with the caveat "more data is required". — [NTRS 20040034179](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040034179)
- **Expert statement [OPENED]** Stone & Thompson 1992 (TM): contrast-induced speed misperception may matter "if pilots use night-vision equipment that produces low-contrast imagery." — [NTRS 19930009915](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930009915)
- **Expert consensus [OPENED]** Statler (ed.) 1981: "The importance of resolution is so apparent that a few words of caution are advisable. Resolution and contrast are interrelated." Welch's companion statement that image detail and resolution "seem to be significant in determining pilot performance in a simulator" is known only from a search extract [not opened]. — [NTRS 19810012531](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19810012531)

### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** The direction "too little resolution/contrast hurts" is well attested; the direction "more than natural sharpness helps" is not attested at all in what could be read.

### Gaps
- NTRS full-text search returned no hits for NVG resolution vs altitude estimation, image sharpening and pilot performance, or FLIR edge enhancement. DTIC, where such reports (AFRL, USAARL) would be, was blocked. This question is effectively unanswered.

## 3. X-Plane: Laminar's own statements on detail textures (decals) and orthophotos

### Takeaway
Laminar's documented answer to "ground texture lacks detail up close" is a repeating high-frequency detail texture blended into the albedo, designed to fade to "no effect" with distance. No Laminar page that could be opened recommends sharpening orthophotos or gives a metres-per-pixel recommendation for them.

### Cited Findings
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** "Using Decals to Add Detail To Scenery", developer.x-plane.com, last updated May 30, 2014: "Decals are overlay textures that sit on top of a regular albedo (day-time) texture to add high-frequency, high-res detail to an existing texture." "Decals work by modulating the textures they overlay. Decals typically repeat much faster than the base texture". "Decals contain high frequency pixels at high res; when viewed from far away, the entire decal blends together into a constant color/grayscale. The decal shader ensures that when a decal has become a solid tone (because it is far away) the net result is the same as your underlying albedo. In other words, as you move away from the decal, the decal blurs into 'no effect'." Greyscale decals "are applied using a blending mode similar to Photoshop's 'hard light' mode." Example: `DECAL 25.0 ..textures/soil/DECAL_stony_dirt.png` applies the decal "at 25.0 x the frequency of the albedo, in other words, it will repeat every 60 metres." Decals are allowed "in any file that uses the Standard Shader, including .ter, .pol, and .net files." — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/article/using-decals-to-add-detail-to-scenery/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** "Standard Shading Options for X-Plane Scenery", last updated October 10, 2024: "The decal stage blends a higher resolution repeating texture into a base texture to create the illusion of high resolution. Because the decal is part of the shading process (and not an additional layer on top of the terrain), the performance impact is quite low." "[X-Plane 12.1.0+] The decals and newly added normal map decals can also be used in .obj files." "Shaders can use up to two decal commands". — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/article/standard-shading-options-for-x-plane-10-scenery/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** "Detail Textures for Aircraft", last updated July 18, 2024: "Detail Textures were previously referred to as 'DECALS'. The term DECAL is a legacy term." "They were designed to add higher density textural detail without having to resort to large texel densities to capture those small details. Detail Textures were designed for use with ground textures but adapted for aircraft use". — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/article/detail-textures-for-aircraft/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** Ben Supnik, "A Few Notes on 12.1.0 for Developers", posted February 24, 2024: "The material system now features normal-map decals. These decals add high-frequency 'texture' to a surface by perturbing the normals (direction of light bounce) instead of just 'painting' them." The text of this post as I read it does not mention RCAS or sharpening; the round-1 statement that 12.1 includes an RCAS sharpening pass is **not confirmed by this page** (it may be on the news blog, which I did not open). — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/2024/02/a-few-notes-on-12-1-0-for-developers/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT / OPINION [OPENED]** Ben Supnik, "Orthophotos Are Not Going Away", posted October 6, 2010. Quotes Austin Meyer: "Orthophotos are garbage. ... I am zooming along in an airplane looking that rooftops of WalMarts painted flat onto the ground. And the rooftops are blurry. And pixelated." Supnik's position: "Orthophotos of any reasonable quality are too large for covering the entire world in the base X-Plane product"; "X-Plane will page orthophotos on multiple cores so that you get smooth flight and crisp images"; "orthophotos can look very good when they are combined with 3-d in a correlated way ... putting matching 3-d on top of it, which makes things look good close up." — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/2010/10/orthophotos-are-not-going-away/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** Ben Supnik, "The Limits of Orthophotos and Meshes in X-Plane", posted February 6, 2008: "X-Plane has no limits on how the texturing is applied – that is, you can use your 2048×2048 texture to cover an entire tile or a single meter. So again, the limiting factor on the resolution of your orthophotos is how much total area you want to cover and how much RAM you can spend". (The page prints "2028×2048", evidently a typo.) — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/2008/02/the-limits-of-orthophotos-and-meshes-in-x-plane/)
- **VENDOR STATEMENT [OPENED]** Laminar Research (byline link `author/justsid`), "Fighting blurry textures", posted May 19, 2020 (X-Plane 11.50 Vulkan/Metal): "when you run out of VRAM, you get blurry textures"; "we have blurry textures because the alternative is guaranteed stutters." This concerns the texture pager lowering resolution under VRAM pressure, a different cause of blur from the orthophoto's native resolution. (The three Supnik posts above carry the byline link `author/bsupnik`.) — [developer.x-plane.com](https://developer.x-plane.com/2020/05/fighting-blurry-textures/)

### Inferences
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Laminar's mechanism matches the simulator literature's remedy (Sweet & Kaiser: "additional layers of finer texture"; Johnson: constant optical density): it adds new texture elements at sub-texel scale near the viewer and fades out with distance by construction, so it cannot shimmer in the far field. Unsharp masking adds no elements and has no built-in distance fade unless it is confined to the top mip level.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** Because X-Plane's pager can drop the top mip level under VRAM pressure ("Fighting blurry textures"), a sharpening applied only to mip 0 would silently disappear whenever the pager withholds that level; the visible result would vary between sessions and hardware.
- **OWN INFERENCE.** A `DECAL` line in the `.ter` files of an ortho package is the Laminar-sanctioned way to add near-field detail over orthophotos; whether XEarthLayer's packages' `.ter` files carry one was not examined in this research task.

### Gaps
- No Laminar text was found that explains why orthophotos look blurry up close in terms of metres per pixel, or that advises on ortho resolution. A search extract mentioned a Supnik remark about roughly 4 m/pixel terrain textures; I did not open that page and do not cite it.
- The X-Plane 12.1 news-blog post (RCAS claim from round 1) was not opened.
- No Laminar statement on sharpening textures at build time was found.
